## 1. CAM and CNC: toolpaths, post-processing for Denford routers, two-sided machining and fixtures, machining times and failures

### Takeaway
The car body is still machined on a school CNC router, almost always a Denford (the official equipment supplier), from the official polyurethane "Model Block" (223 x 65 x 50 mm). Development and Entry cars must be cut in two operations, the right side and then the left, with the block flipped 180 degrees on the Denford F1 fixture. The datum is set at the centre of the CO2 / "power pack" hole on the rear face. Toolpaths come either from Denford's QuickCAM Pro (6 mm ball-nose default, wizard "plans") or from the Fusion Manufacture workspace, using the Denford post from Autodesk's Post Library and a STEM Racing tool library for Denford machines. I found no published machining times, feeds and speeds, or first-hand failure reports.

**Two-sided machining, datum and flipping**
- Three-axis CNC is the minimum for body manufacture. **Entry and Development class cars may only be machined as a left side and a right side**, so every machined surface must be visible in side view. Undercuts are not feasible "without special equipment or imaginative fixturing" **[current]** — [Yas in Schools Torque: CNC Manufacturing Limitations](https://support.yasinschools.com/stem-racing/manufacturing/cnc-manufacturing-limitations/)
- The standard method is two machining operations. The right side is cut first; the block is then **rotated 180 degrees about the X axis** and the left side (a mirror image) is cut **[older tutorial, Inventor-based]** — [F1 in Schools Car Design Advanced Tutorial (PDF)](https://energiazero.org/cartelle/disegno_inventor/F1/tutorial%20F1/Tutorial_F1_Advanced_Inventor.pdf)
- Fixture datum: the origin sits at **the centre of the CO2 hole on the back face of the block**. The default datum in the software is front-left-top of the block, so it has to be changed to match the car model's origin. A fixture program is run before machining so the billet is held securely. Programs also exist to **drill the axle holes** in the block — [Denford forum: F1 Fixture Setup](https://denfordata.com/bb/viewtopic.php?f=61&t=6077&p=24737); [Scribd: F1 Offsets for the Denford CO2 Fixture](https://www.scribd.com/document/86203008/F1-Offsets-for-the-Denford-Co2-Fixture-1); [REA F1 Fixture tutorial](https://learning.rea.org.au/Guide/F1+Fixture+-+Side+Machining+-+QuickCAM+Pro+-+F1+in+Schools+Car+Tutorial/121)
- Axle-guide holes are drilled during the CNC machining process (Entry class guidance) **[current]** — [STEM Racing Entry Competition Guidebook 2025-2026](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6913005a89f25e1a2f5bcef5/t/692ec6999b44cc201847e7c6/1764673177837/stem_racing_entry_competition_guidebook_2025_-_2026+(1).pdf)
- In India, STEMplify, which sells under a "STEM RACING™ INDIA" banner, lists "Car Body Manufacturing with Model Block" as a product, i.e. a paid body-machining service for teams (reported as on a Denford 1000). This shows some teams outsource CNC work **[date unclear]** — [STEMplify: Car Body Manufacturing with Model Block](https://www.stemplify.com/product/car-body-manufacturing-f1-in-schools-india/)

**QuickCAM Pro / VR Milling route (Denford's own software)**
- QuickCAM Pro is a wizard-based CAM package that imports STL and image files. It has **12 machining plans: 3 roughing, 6 finishing and 3 fine-finishing**. Plans can be combined and each can use a different cutter — [Denford QuickCAM Pro datasheet](https://www.amtekcompany.com/pdf/datasheets/Denford/Denford_quickcam-pro.pdf); [Denford: QuickCAM Pro](https://denford.co.uk/products/quickcam-pro-software)

Reducing the **stepover** gives a better finish but a longer run — [REA F1 Fixture tutorial](https://learning.rea.org.au/Guide/F1+Fixture+-+Side+Machining+-+QuickCAM+Pro+-+F1+in+Schools+Car+Tutorial/121); [Scribd: QuickCAM Pro F1 in Schools Training Guide](https://www.scribd.com/document/821581606/QuickCAM-Pro-F1-in-Schools-Training-Guide); [Issuu: QuickCAM Pro F1 Training Guide (F1 in Schools Thailand)](https://issuu.com/f1inschoolsthailand/docs/quickcam_pro_f1)

(The search summary did not say which of the two sources this came from.) — [Denford brochure (Farnell)](https://www.farnell.com/datasheets/1858485.pdf); [REA 4th Axis tutorial](https://learning.rea.org.au/Guide/4th+Axis+-+Side+Machining+-+QuickCAM+Pro+-+F1+in+Schools+Car+Tutorial/119)

- Denford posts are in the Autodesk Post Library (search "denford"). A Denford F1 in Schools post for Autodesk is referenced. ### Inferences
- The typical school sequence is: export the body or open it in Fusion Manufacture. Set up stock equal to the 223 x 65 x 50 mm block, with the WCS on the centre of the rear CO2 / power-pack hole so it matches the Denford fixture datum. Rough, then finish with a ball-nose cutter (6 mm in QuickCAM by default) on side one. Flip 180 degrees about X on the fixture and repeat on side two. Run the axle-hole drilling program. The datum-at-CO2-hole convention is what lets the flipped block register without separate dowel pins, since the fixture locates on that hole. This is an inference from the fixture datum description; I did not find a description of pin geometry.
- The left/right-only rule for Entry and Development classes means designers must avoid undercuts in side view. That pushes overhanging features (wings, nose tips, halo details) onto separately made, usually 3D-printed, parts.
- Stepover is the main lever teams have on finish quality versus machine time. Since sanding and filling follow anyway, schools with limited machine time probably use coarser finishing stepovers. This is inferred; no numbers were found.

## 2. 3D printing: which parts, printer types, settings, post-processing, round wheels and bearing seats, and print sponsors

### Takeaway
Everything except the foam body is commonly 3D printed: wings and nose pieces, wheel supports, tether-guide parts and (increasingly) wheels. Budget teams use FDM PLA and fight seams, poor roundness and imbalance. Stronger teams move to resin (SLA) or "advanced engineering plastics", or use machined wheels (acetal or aluminium) with ceramic bearings. The 2026 rules add wheel safety tests and an optional wheel "Pit Stop" challenge, a sign that wheel breakage and glued-on wheels have been a real problem. I found no first-hand STEM Racing print settings (layer height, orientation, cure time).

### Cited Findings
- Team Ignite (a STEM Racing team; the "STEM Racing" name suggests the rebranded era, so recent) printed wheels **on home printers in PLA** for its regional event. The wheels had problems, including **no smooth surface and visible seams, which caused unbalanced weight**. The team wants **CNC-machined or 3D-printed PEEK wheels and PETG wheel supports** and sought PCBWay sponsorship **[current, exact date unknown]** — [PCBWay: Team Ignite STEM Racing Car Wheel System](https://www.pcbway.com/project/sponsor/Team_Ignite_STEM_Racing_Car_Wheel_System_e649b6de.html)
- Forum advice: "3D printing cannot print circles very well" and lathe-made wheels get "much better results". One team's **solid PLA wheels had to be sanded thoroughly because of the printer's 0.2 mm resolution**. Photopolymer (resin) wheels have also been used. **Acetal** is recommended as light, strong enough and low-friction **[older forum]** — [F1technical: F1 in Schools Wheel System](https://www.f1technical.net/forum/viewtopic.php?f=14&t=21775)
- Wheels are described as either 3D printed in durable polymers or **CNC-machined aluminium** for strength and consistency. Each wheel "should be carefully tested for symmetry, balance, and optimal fit with the axle" (the search summary attributed this to a shared project page) — [PCBWay: F1 in schools front wheel project](https://www.pcbway.com/project/shareproject/F1_in_schools_front_whell_4fb3069b.html)
- Bearing seats: teams struggle to choose press-fit tolerances (housing to bearing OD, shaft to bearing ID). Plastics "deform plastically long before a press fit really starts working" as it does in metal. One team considered ABS/acetal housings with acetal shafts **[older forum]** — [F1technical: wheel dimensions and bearing fitting](https://www.f1technical.net/forum/viewtopic.php?t=27104); [F1technical: tolerance for bearing in aluminium](https://www.f1technical.net/forum/viewtopic.php?t=24072)
- Ultimaker 2+ FDM case (CREATE Education sponsorship): the printer made **every component except the foam chassis**. **Wheels were printed hollow**, because infill or support would make them non-axisymmetric and slower, so rotating parts weighed **under 1 g**. The **rear-wing module was printed as three separate parts** to get the smoothest aerofoil surfaces. The hollow-wheel and three-part-wing details came from a search summary that also listed the LEAF1 Printables page, so the attribution is probable, not certain **[older; Ultimaker 2+ era, likely c. 2016-18]** — [CREATE Education: Formula 1 in Schools update](https://www.createeducation.com/blog/fm-1-team-racing-formula-1-schools-update/)
- Hydron (2022 World Champions): "The car is made from foam, with **3D printed parts** and **wheels manufactured from advanced engineering plastics**" **[older, 2022]** — [Off The Track: Hydron in their own words](https://offthetrack.net/news/hydron-in-their-own-words/)
- A team page describes the split: body machined from an expanded polyurethane block on CNC, **wings made on a 3D printer**, and bearings and eyelets **purchased** — [Pocadores: F1 in Schools](https://www.pocadores.com/f1inschools)
- A Malaysia–China collaboration team reportedly developed a new plastic to build its wings and wheels (coverage of an Aramco-sponsored F1 in Schools World Finals) **[date unclear, pre-2024 "F1 in Schools" branding]** — [Formula1.com: Youngsters go for glory in Aramco F1 in Schools World Finals](https://www.formula1.com/en/latest/article/youngsters-go-for-glory-in-aramco-f1-in-schools-world-finals.1JOS288vrdy6hojRlmKfqe)
- Teams share printable STL sets for their season parts, for example LEAF1 @ Langton's 2022/23 season upload — [Printables: LEAF1 F1 in Schools 2022/2023](https://www.printables.com/model/381830-leaf1-f1-in-schools-20222023-season-upload)
- Commercial wheel options exist for teams that do not make their own, for example RACE2LEARN's "Competition STEM Racing Wheels (4 pack)" (US) — [RACE2LEARN: STEM Racing Wheels](https://race2learn.net/products/race2learn-performance-stem-racing-wheels)
- 2026 World Finals: **revised safety tests for wheels**, plus a new optional **Pit Stop Challenge** on the wheel–axle interface. Its stated aim is **reducing wheel breakage** and letting teams swap a damaged wheel. Mounting must be reusable and non-destructive, **without adhesives or permanent fixings** (pins, fasteners, positive interlocks), and **one wheel change must take under 60 s**. There is a Pit Stop Award **[current]** — [STEM Racing: Understanding the 2026 Technical Regulations](https://www.stemracing.com/news/aramco-stem-racing-world-finals-2026-understanding-the-technical-regulations); [SRWF26 Technical Regulations PDF (uploaded 10 Feb 2026)](https://www.stemracing.com/s/SRWF26_Technical_Regulations-1.pdf)
- (Not STEM Racing.) SLS Nylon 12 sponsorship examples found were for **Formula Student** (3DPRINTUK and TBR21), not STEM Racing teams — [3DPRINTUK case study](https://www.3dprint-uk.co.uk/case-study/3dprintuk-sponsors-tbr21-formula-student-automotive-vision/)

### Inferences
- The progression most teams report runs: FDM PLA wheels (cheap, but seams, imbalance and poor roundness) → resin or engineering-plastic prints, or turned or machined wheels (acetal, aluminium), plus bought ceramic bearings → sponsor-made parts (PEEK, industrial printing). Budget and sponsor access largely decide how far a team gets.
- The 2026 Pit Stop / no-adhesive wheel mounting and new wheel safety tests suggest the common failure modes are wheels that break on launch or at the catch, and wheels glued in place that cannot be replaced at events. Teams designing for 2026 will need printed or machined hubs with mechanical retention and tighter bearing-seat tolerances.

## 3. Finishing: filling, sanding, primers, paint, decals and vinyl, lacquer, added mass and surface smoothness

### Takeaway
The finishing sequence teams describe is: sand and file after machining, apply a thin skin of filler or putty, re-sand smooth, hand spray paint, apply decals, then clear coat or varnish over the decals. Every source treats paint as a mass risk ("very heavy"). No STEM Racing source in this search gave grit sequences, primer products or measured paint mass for a current car.

### Cited Findings
- Process described in an F1 in Schools prep guide: after milling, cars are **sanded and filed**, coated with **a thin layer of putty**, and sanded again until completely smooth. Teams **spray paint by hand**, which one team called "nerve-wracking" because paint is "very heavy and can drastically affect the weight and speed of the car". Cars are finished with **varnish for a glossy look** **[older, undated]**. The search summary did not say which page it drew on; Boca Bearings' guide is the most likely source — [Boca Bearings: How to prepare for a F1 in Schools competition](https://www.bocabearings.com/f1-school-bearing-tips-tutorials/how-to-prepare-for-a-f1-in-schools-competition)
- Decal practice (from an RC-model painting guide, **not STEM Racing**): apply decals over paint, then spray clear coat over them for strength and to stop cracking and peeling. The guide uses FAScolour for airbrushing and Spaz Stix and Tamiya aerosols for larger areas — [RC Formula 1 Australia: Painting Guide Part 3](https://www.rcformula1.com.au/index.php/43-modelling/painting/700-painting-guide-part-3)
- Model Block is marketed as "easy to paint" with a very fine surface structure — [Technology Supplies: F1 Model Block](https://www.technologysupplies.co.uk/f1-model-block.html); [Denford Model Block datasheet](https://denfordata.com/downloads/F1%20Model%20Block%20Technical%20Data%20Sheet%2029.6.15.pdf)
- Mass anecdote **[older forum, pre-polyurethane-block era likely]**: one team's body weighed **15.32 g without wheels, axles or other parts**, and the car was **~35 g in total after painting**. The ~20 g gap covers wheels, axles and fittings as well as paint, so it is **not** a measure of paint mass alone — [F1technical: F1 in Schools Aero Racing help](https://www.f1technical.net/forum/viewtopic.php?t=10977)
- The organiser acknowledged the effort in 2023: "We know it's hard work getting that paint job..." — [F1 in Schools HQ Instagram (2023)](https://www.instagram.com/f1inschoolshq/p/CqUl891Mctt/)
- Teams design and share paint stands for holding a car while spraying — [GrabCAD: F1 in Schools car paint stand](https://grabcad.com/library/f1-in-schools-car-paint-stand-1)
- Current mass context: the **absolute minimum car mass is 60.0 g** (World Finals 2025/26 and Development 2025-26 regulations). Legal **ballast** is allowed only in the container under the halo, reached through the halo/helmet aperture. Fishing lead weights or similar are acceptable. If a car is found underweight, **0.2 g of ballast is added for every 0.1 g underweight** before racing **[current]** — [SRWF26 Technical Regulations](https://www.stemracing.com/s/SRWF26_Technical_Regulations-1.pdf); [SR World Finals 2025 Technical Regulations](https://www.stemracing.com/uploads/1/1/8/9/118908723/sr_wf_2025_techregs_01042025.pdf); [Development Technical Regulations 2025-2026](https://www.stemracing.co.uk/uploads/1/1/8/9/118908723/stem_racing_development_technical_regulations_2025-2026.pdf). **Unreconciled:** another snippet said the 2025 changes included "a **reduction** in minimum car weight and the introduction of legal ballast". I could not confirm the previous value from these sources — [SR World Finals 2025 regs (Brazil mirror)](https://stemracingbrazil.com.br/files/Regulamentos%20T%C3%A9cnicos%20Classe%20Profissional%20para%20Final%20Mundial%202025.pdf)
- Proportional penalties (the "PP+" rules) round up to the next whole unit (**1.0 mm or 1.0 g**) and apply once per whole unit outside the limit **[current]** — [Development Technical Regulations 2025-2026](https://www.stemracing.co.uk/uploads/1/1/8/9/118908723/stem_racing_development_technical_regulations_2025-2026.pdf)

### Inferences
- With a hard 60.0 g minimum, ballast allowed only under the halo, and a 2:1 penalty ballast for being underweight, the practical target is a finished car slightly under 60 g before ballast, trimmed to exactly 60.0 g. Paint and filler mass therefore matter less as "speed" than as a budget to control, and teams must weigh the car after finishing, not only in CAD.
- The finishing steps (filler, sanding, primer, colour, decals, clear) are where the two race cars can drift apart in mass and surface quality. Teams that weigh and record mass at each stage have the evidence they need for the portfolio's "finishing techniques" testing. (Inferred from the handbook's requirement to "research and test finishing techniques", see section 6.)

### Gaps
- **No STEM Racing-specific grit sequence, primer, filler product, paint system, vinyl vs water-slide decal choice, or lacquer** was found for 2023-26. Generic automotive advice (180-220 → 320-400 → 400-600 grit, wet sanding at refinement) appeared in results but is **not** from STEM Racing sources — [generic sanding blog](https://blog.equalle.com/posts/2026/06/08/block-sanding-primer-coats-before-paint-sanding-block/).
- **No measured paint or filler mass (g)** for a current car was found. No surface-roughness data or drag-vs-finish test results were found either.

## 4. Assembly: bearings, axles, wheel alignment, tether line guides, gluing wings and nose, jigs, weighing and ballast, A and B cars and spares

### Takeaway
Assembly centres on low-friction wheel systems (bought silicon-nitride ceramic or hybrid bearings, often "sandwiched" in the hub to remove side play) and on tether line guides. Entry cars use two standard screw-eye guides; higher classes may use team-made guides. The 2026 halo acts as a standard structure and the only legal ballast location. Wings are commonly printed separately and bonded with superglue. I found nothing on alignment jigs or on how teams match their A and B cars.

### Cited Findings
- Entry class must use **two standard tether-line-guide screw eyes** (from the Entry starter kit) fixed into the underside of the body. A tether line guide "completely surrounds the track tether line". Guides may be bought or made wholly or partly by the team. Entry cars must use **two standard axles and axle bushes, modified only in length**, and **axle guides insert into holes drilled during CNC machining** **[current, guidebook uploaded 2 Dec 2025]** — [STEM Racing Entry Competition Guidebook 2025-2026](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6913005a89f25e1a2f5bcef5/t/692ec6999b44cc201847e7c6/1764673177837/stem_racing_entry_competition_guidebook_2025_-_2026+(1).pdf); [F1 in Schools N.A. Development Technical Regulations 2024-25](https://f1inschoolsna.squarespace.com/s/F1iS-DEVCLASS_NATechnicalRegulations2024-25v2.pdf)

- Wings can be designed for separate 3D printing and **attached with adhesives such as superglue**. This is from a search summary whose exact source was not identified; the candidates were the Yas in Schools Torque pages returned in the same search — [Yas in Schools Torque: content list](https://f1-in-schools-torque.invisionzone.com/profile/1-yas-in-schools/content/?type=forums_topic_post); [Yas in Schools Torque: Research & Investigate](https://f1-in-schools-torque.invisionzone.com/al-dhafra/research-investigate/)
- Bearings: **silicon nitride full-ceramic radial bearings, typically 7 mm diameter**, are used for minimal rolling resistance. Some teams pick **stainless-ceramic hybrids** after testing with bearing makers. A "sandwiching" hub design holds each side of the bearing to **remove side-to-side play** so the wheel runs true **[older, 2018]** — [Boca Bearings: F1 in Schools bearing and wheel design](https://www.bocabearings.com/blogs/2018/f1-in-schools-bearing-and-wheel-design); [SMB Bearings supports F1 in Schools national champions](https://www.smbbearings.com/smb-bearings-support-f1-in-schools-national-champions)

- 2026 World Finals: the **Halo is a standard structure on all cars**, introduced at the Indian and Australian National Finals in 2025. The regulations also describe a new standard **deceleration system that uses a rubber band to catch the car after the finish line**. (The summary may have run the halo and the deceleration system together.) **Ballast may only go in the container under the halo** **[current]** — [STEM Racing: Understanding the 2026 Technical Regulations](https://www.stemracing.com/news/aramco-stem-racing-world-finals-2026-understanding-the-technical-regulations); [SRWF26 Technical Regulations](https://www.stemracing.com/s/SRWF26_Technical_Regulations-1.pdf)

### Inferences
- The 2026 no-adhesive wheel challenge and the ballast-only-under-halo rule will change assembly practice. Superglued wheels and glued ballast in arbitrary places give way to designed hub retention and a single ballast pocket, which makes A/B car matching and trackside wheel swaps easier.
- Because axle holes are drilled by the same CNC program on both cars, axle alignment is set mainly by the machining setup (datum on the CO2 hole) and not by a hand jig. This is inferred from the Denford fixture and axle-hole drilling descriptions, not directly documented.

## 5. Testing: tracks, launch systems, timing, logged data, wind and smoke tunnels, friction and spin tests, number of runs, analysis and feedback into design

### Takeaway
The standard kit is Denford's race system: a 20 m race over a roughly 24 m track (elevated 23.65 m or roll-out 24 m), a solenoid-released launch pod, IR start and finish gates, reaction-time modes and optional sector gates. Since June 2025 there is also an official Denford/Matrix benchtop wind tunnel with smoke. Top teams report that track timing is noisy (Hydron measured about 20 % variation in CO2 canister force output) and rely on averaging, standard deviation and simulation. The 2025 World Finals fastest car ran **1.118 s**. I found no formal roll-down or spin-down test protocols.

### Cited Findings
**Tracks, launch and timing hardware**
- Denford F1 Race System: **11 track pieces and 12 legs make a 23.65 m track**, supplied with the launch pod and start/finish gates with timing control. The launch pod has a **hardened silver-steel firing pin**, a **torsion spring latched under pressure** for consistent release, a **tool-steel latch** to resist wear, and a powerful **release solenoid** — [Denford F1 Race System User Guide](https://denfordata.com/downloads/F1RaceSystemUserManual(2101021).pdf); [Denford: Race Package, Elevated Track](https://denford.co.uk/products/stem-racing-race-system-package-halo)
- The Race Control System has **Auto, Manual and Reaction Time modes**, a **28-inch display** and Wi-Fi for live race data including reaction times. The finish line uses **frequency-encoded infra-red** emitters and receivers built into the track. **Additional sector timing gates** record split times for "car acceleration and deceleration graphs" **[current]** — [Denford: Race Control System](https://denford.co.uk/products/stem-racing-race-system-package-stand-alone); [Denford: Sector Gate Timing System](https://denford.co.uk/shop/sector-gate-timing-system)
- The entry-level **Roll Out Track** package is a portable **24 m roll-out track** with precision timing and start/finish gates. It is built to order and quoted individually (no price published). UK schools can also buy through the D&T Association **[current]** — [Denford: Race Package, Roll Out Track](https://denford.co.uk/products/stem-racing-race-system-package-roll-out); [D&T Association resource shop](https://www.designtechnology.org.uk/resource-shop/f1-race-system-package-roll-out-race-track/)
- Denford has launched a **four-lane** STEM Racing track for large-scale education use **[date not visible in snippet; "STEM Racing" branding implies recent]** — [ETIH EdTech News](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/denford-launches-four-lane-stem-racing-track-for-large-scale-education-use)
- Race basics: two cars race head-to-head over **20 metres**, each powered by a single gas cartridge — [STEM Racing UK](https://www.stemracing.co.uk/). Cars use an **8 g CO2 cartridge** pierced by the launcher — [Wikipedia: STEM Racing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STEM_Racing); [UiTM paper PDF](https://ir.uitm.edu.my/93573/1/93573.pdf). Current Denford listings call the cartridge the "Power Pack" (18 mm hole) — [Denford shop](https://denford.co.uk/shop/stem-racing-model-block-blank-single)
- Speed claims conflict: "speeds topping **80 km/h** in less than a second" — [LEAP Australia on Hydron](https://www.leapaust.com.au/blog/cfd/congratulations-hydron-2022-f1-in-schools-world-champions/); versus "up to **95 km/h**" — [Boca Bearings](https://www.bocabearings.com/blogs/2018/f1-in-schools-bearing-and-wheel-design)
- Benchmark: **Lunar** (Brighton Grammar, Melbourne) won the **2025 World Finals in Singapore**, including the Fastest Car Award with **1.118 s**, against 82 other teams from 34 countries **[current]** — [STEM Racing: Lunar crowned 2025 World Champions](https://www.stemracing.com/news/lunar-crowned-aramco-stem-racing-world-champions-2025); [Brighton Grammar](https://www.brightongrammar.vic.edu.au/lunar-crowned-2025-aramco-stem-racing-world-champions/); [Raceteq](https://www.raceteq.com/articles/2025/10/2025-aramco-stem-racing-world-finals-singapore)

**Wind tunnel, smoke and CFD**
- Official **STEM Racing Wind Tunnel** (Matrix TSL, Denford and STEM Racing partnership): a compact, open-circuit, subsonic **benchtop** tunnel with a **smoke machine** for streamlines, a computer-controlled fan, data acquisition, a touch-screen interface and **over 35 m/s** airspeed **[current, announced 2 June 2025]** (date decoded from the LinkedIn post ID) — [STEM Racing: partnership with Matrix TSL](https://www.stemracing.com/news/new-strategic-partnership-with-matrix-tsl); [Denford: Wind Tunnel](https://denford.co.uk/products/stem-racing-wind-tunnel); [LinkedIn launch post](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/stemracing_just-launched-the-official-stem-racing-wind-activity-7335279083863384064-ogA-)
- Denford also sells **Virtual Wind Tunnel** CFD software for schools — [Denford: Virtual Wind Tunnel](https://denford.co.uk/products/virtual-wind-tunnel-software-2)
- An academic case study (Malaysia, UiTM) combined CFD with wind-tunnel testing of an F1 in Schools car and reported a **9.89 % drag reduction** from its modifications — [JAEDS: Optimising F1 in Schools car design using CFD and wind tunnel testing](https://jaeds.uitm.edu.my/index.php/jaeds/article/view/77)
- Teams have used university tunnels: one team tested **three designs for every component** and put **three prototypes in the wind tunnel at Imperial College London**. Teams also **print physical prototypes, race them and analyse high-speed footage** to judge dynamic behaviour, using CFD to improve "from one test day to the next". This comes from a search summary; the specific page among the candidates below was not identified — [Ansys blog: F1 in Schools, inspiring future engineers](https://www.ansys.com/blog/formula-1-student-team); [Autodesk Fusion blog: Team Nightingale 2023](https://www.autodesk.com/products/fusion-360/blog/team-nightingale-f1-in-schools-world-finals-2023/); [Team Aqueous: About F1 in Schools](https://teamaqueous.com/about-f1-in-schools)

**Noise, variance and how data are analysed**
- Hydron (2022 champions) engineering portfolio: testing the **force output of CO2 canisters showed a 20 % error range**, so "physically track testing prototypes for times was determined to be an inaccurate and inconsistent method of design evaluation" **[older, 2022]** — [Hydron Design & Engineering Portfolio (PDF)](https://offthetrack.net/img/t41_hydron_australia_engineering_compressed.pdf)
- Hydron built a **900-line Python "Physics Race Model"** and iterated the aerodynamics continuously, reporting a **15.61 % improvement from first prototype to final design** — [Off The Track: Hydron in their own words](https://offthetrack.net/news/hydron-in-their-own-words/)
- REA's teacher guide (Australia) says students calculate the **average run time and standard deviation** of test results to justify design decisions and car selection. To get accurate results they should remove or reduce variables: **weather conditions, canister weights, car weight and track set-up**. The variables list came from the same search summary, which also returned team portfolios, so it may originate in a portfolio and not the REA guide — [REA: A guide to running F1 in Schools in Australia v1.0](https://rea.org.au/wp-content/uploads/Getting-Started-V1.0.pdf); [Hydron portfolio](https://offthetrack.net/img/t41_hydron_australia_engineering_compressed.pdf)
- The canister is said to push the car for only about **one third of the track length**, and consistency across heats, not one standout time, usually decides winners. The search summary drew on portfolio and academic sources but did not identify which — [Infinitude engineering portfolio 2016](https://offthetrack.net/img/infinitude-australia_engineering.pdf); [UiTM paper PDF](https://ir.uitm.edu.my/93573/1/93573.pdf)
- Safety history: the Launch Energy Recovery System (LERS) with extended canister chambers, first used in 2014 by Colossus F1, was **banned from the 2017 World Finals season** after safety issues **[older]** — [Wikipedia: STEM Racing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STEM_Racing)

### Inferences
- A school with a Denford track can log per run: race time, reaction time (Reaction Time mode), sector splits (with extra gates), and, by hand, car mass, canister mass or batch, and conditions. The REA list of variables to control (weather, canister weights, car weight, track set-up) is effectively the standard test-log schema.
- Given a reported ~20 % spread in canister output, a single run tells a team almost nothing. Teams need several runs per configuration, an average and SD, and controlled canister selection (e.g. weighing canisters) to detect the few-percent gains that aero or wheel changes produce. Hydron's answer was to move design evaluation into simulation (CFD plus a physics race model) and use track runs for validation.
- The new sector gates (acceleration and deceleration graphs), the benchtop tunnel with smoke (2025) and the halo catch system (2025-26) mean current teams can separate launch or thrust effects from drag and friction effects more cleanly than pre-2023 teams, if their school or region owns the kit.

### Gaps
- **No formal roll-down, coast-down or wheel spin-down test protocols** (ramp angle, spin-time targets) were found in snippets, although bearing-friction work is widely mentioned.
- **No timing apps** (phone or slow-motion apps) used by schools without Denford gates were documented.
- **Typical number of test runs per session or per season**, and real run-to-run SD values for a single car (e.g. ± ms), were not found.
- The race-format rule on how reaction time combines with race time in knockouts was not retrieved (search budget exhausted).
- Details of how often schools actually have track access (own track vs regional hubs vs event-day only) were not found. Prices for Denford tracks and the wind tunnel are quote-only.

## 6. Cycle length: how long a design-make-test iteration takes, how many car versions a team builds per season, and how iterations are documented for the portfolio

### Takeaway
No source gave a per-iteration duration for a school team. The evidence shows that champion teams are multi-season projects: Hydron took about 3 years and 6 state and national events to win Worlds, and Lunar entered in 2022 and won in 2025. Within a season, top teams iterate heavily in CAD and CFD and build a limited number of physical prototypes, with a measurable gain from first prototype to final (Hydron: 15.61 %). Portfolios are expected to document CFD-driven changes, physical prototypes and testing, further development, and finishing-technique tests.

### Cited Findings
- Hydron (2022 World Champions): the effort began **four years earlier as two separate Development Class teams**, and the win was the "culmination of **3 years of work**", including **six State and National competitions** before Worlds. They reported **15.61 % improvement from first prototype to final design** **[older, 2022]** — [Off The Track: Hydron in their own words](https://offthetrack.net/news/hydron-in-their-own-words/); [LEAP Australia](https://www.leapaust.com.au/blog/cfd/congratulations-hydron-2022-f1-in-schools-world-champions/)
- Lunar has competed since **2022** and became World Champions in **2025**. In their process "every component or section was explored with multiple elaborate and innovative concepts". After the **2025 regulation changes** they **tested numerous front-wing designs** in Fusion before choosing an "extremely low profile" wing **[current]** — [Lunar: Our story](https://www.lunaraus.com/story); [STEM Racing: How Lunar used Ansys & Autodesk](https://www.stemracing.com/news/how-lunar-used-ansys-amp-autodesk-to-become-our-world-champions)
- Handbook expectations (2024-25): design in 3D CAD, **test with CFD and improve**, **manufacture prototypes and do physical testing**, develop the design further, and **research and test finishing techniques**. The portfolios summarise all of this — [F1 in Schools Handbook 2024-25 (Scribd)](https://www.scribd.com/document/856497607/F1-in-Schools-HandBook)
- The engineering portfolio should present design ideas, development, research, testing and evaluation. Development and Professional class teams submit A3 enterprise and engineering portfolios — [REA: Getting Started guide](https://rea.org.au/wp-content/uploads/Getting-Started-V1.0.pdf); [F1 in Schools World Finals 2023 Competition Regulations](https://www.f1inschools.com/uploads/1/1/8/9/118908723/f1swf_2023_competiton_regulations.pdf)
- Teams "test hundreds of designs" using physical and virtual methods, with "a lot of iterations before settling on a few prototypes and moving into final optimization". This is from a search summary; the Ansys blog is the probable source, but the same result set included team pages — [Ansys blog: F1 in Schools](https://www.ansys.com/blog/formula-1-student-team); [Ansys: Role of simulation for winning STEM Racing teams](https://www.ansys.com/blog/role-simulation-winning-teams-stem-racing)
- Public exemplars of how iterations are documented: Hydron's (2022) and Infinitude's (2016) engineering portfolios on Off The Track, and Team Nightingale's 2023 World Finals podium story on the Fusion blog (not readable here) — [Off The Track: Portfolios](https://offthetrack.net/portfolios/); [Autodesk Fusion blog: Team Nightingale 2023](https://www.autodesk.com/products/fusion-360/blog/team-nightingale-f1-in-schools-world-finals-2023/)
- Rules change every season. Development regulations for **2026-2027** were already published (uploaded **1 June 2026**), so the regulations teams build to change year to year — [STEM Racing Development Technical Regulations 2026-2027](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6913005a89f25e1a2f5bcef5/t/6a1d66b2c057810a68895f00/1780311730822/STEM+Racing+Development+Technical+Regulations+2026-2027.pdf)

### Inferences
- A realistic school model: many virtual iterations (CAD plus CFD) per week, with physical make-and-test loops limited by CNC and print access, finishing and drying time, and track access. That points to a handful of physical prototype bodies per season for a typical team, with top teams building more. This is inferred from the "few prototypes" language and the machine and track constraints; **no count was found**.
- Because physical track data are noisy (±20 % canister output), top teams in the portfolio pair each design change with a simulated gain (CFD drag, race-model time) and use physical tests to validate the choice, not to rank small differences.

### Gaps
- **No source gave the duration of one design-make-test iteration** (days or weeks) or the **number of car versions or prototypes built per season** by a typical school team.
- No first-hand accounts of school timetabling constraints (lesson-time vs after-school CNC access) were retrievable. Reddit and YouTube were blocked.
- No portfolio templates or tables showing how teams log iteration data (run tables, SD, conditions) could be read in full. The Hydron and Infinitude PDFs are the best leads to open.

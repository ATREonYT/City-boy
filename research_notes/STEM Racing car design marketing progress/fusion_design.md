## 1. Where a design starts: the model block, official CAD templates, "no-go-zone" files, and how they get into Fusion

### Takeaway
Teams rarely start from a truly blank file. The organiser's downloads page offers a Fusion-ready file with the exact Model Block dimensions, plus CAD parts for each class: the block, the body "no-go-zone", halo, helmet, standard wheel, axle and CO2 cartridge. Teams upload these to their Fusion cloud project and model the body around them. The physical block is a polyurethane blank of 223 × 65 × 50 mm, about 112–114 g, with a pre-drilled cartridge hole. Entry, Development and Professional (World Finals) classes each get different files and different freedoms, and they change from season to season.

**Dimensions the CAD must respect (reported through summaries)**
- Power-unit cartridge chamber depth: minimum 45 mm, maximum 58 mm. Tether line guides: internal diameter 3.5–6 mm, and each must pass a 200 g test. ### Inferences
- The typical starting sequence:
  1. Create a Fusion cloud project for the season.
  2. Upload the official files (.f3d where offered, otherwise .stp).
  3. Insert the block, no-go-zone, cartridge, axles, wheels and halo/helmet as fixed reference components.
  4. Model the body as its own component around them.
- In the Development class, teams choose a no-go-zone variant (20 mm or 30 mm offset) and one of four halo platform positions before sculpting, because those choices lock parts of the body envelope.
- The files change each season (new halo, virtual cargo, 80 g Development minimum), so teams should re-download them every season. Carrying over last year's .f3d risks building to an out-of-date envelope.
- In 2025-26 the block (~112–114 g) was much heavier than the Development minimum (60 g). In 2026-27 the 80 g minimum narrows that gap, which may change how aggressively Development teams hollow or slim the body.

### Gaps
- The regulation PDFs could not be opened, so these were not verified: Professional/World Finals minimum weight, overall length/width, wheel diameter and width ranges for 2025-26 and 2026-27, virtual cargo dimensions, exact tether-guide positioning, and cartridge chamber diameter and height above the track.
- It is unclear whether a Professional-class "envelope" or no-go-zone file exists alongside the Entry and Development ones, and in which formats (.f3d or .step).
- The contents of the Autodesk "Getting started with Autodesk Fusion" article on stemracing.com could not be read. It is probably the official import walkthrough.

## 2. How the body is modelled: sketches and constraints, splines, lofts and sweeps, T-splines (Form) versus solid modelling

### Takeaway
Two approaches dominate. **Sculpting (T-splines / Form):** start from the standard car or block, enter the Form environment, and push and pull faces and edges with Edit Form, with symmetry turned on. This is Autodesk's beginner route. **Parametric solid modelling:** sketches on reference planes, then extrude, loft and fillet, cut against the block and checked against no-go-zone bodies modelled from the rules. This is the route taught by World Finals podium team Nightingale's intermediate series. Autodesk's official tutorials run from beginner (customise a standard car) through intermediate (your own design) to advanced (design plus manufacture), all "sculpting from the model block".

### Inferences
- Modelling the regulations first (Off The Track step 2) turns the rulebook into reference bodies, such as no-go-zone, wheel envelopes, wing boxes and clearance zones. The body is then shaped against them instead of being checked after the fact. This appears to be the working habit of top teams, as distinct from beginner template-editing.
- A T-spline body has to become a solid (B-rep) body before it can be combined with the block and no-go-zone and before CAM works on it. Teams therefore usually finish in the solid environment even when they sculpt first. (Inference from how Fusion works; no competition source retrieved.)
- Sculpted bodies make it hard to hold the cartridge chamber, axle holes and tether-guide positions at exact dimensions. Placing those features as parametric cuts after the free-form shape is done is the practical way to keep them in position.

### Gaps
- None of the videos could be watched, so there is no detail on feature order such as loft rails, sweep paths, spline control, or sidepod and underside techniques.
- No source directly describes the use of Sweep, or the "Advanced" Autodesk tutorial's sculpt-from-block steps.

## 3. How the other parts are modelled: wings, nose, wheels and bearings, axles, halo, helmet, virtual cargo; parameters, components, joints, appearances

### Takeaway
Wings and nose are either T-spline sections edited symmetrically (beginner route) or solid lofts of airfoil sketches (intermediate route). Each is kept as a separate component so it can be printed or machined and scrutineered on its own. Standard wheels and axles are mandatory in Entry and UK Development. Professional teams design their own wheels, typically revolved hub, rim and tyre profiles with bearing seats, often 3D printed with ceramic bearings. The halo is a supplied file that sits in a pocket in the body. Assemblies are split into Body, Front Wing, Rear Wing (plus rear wheel support) and Wheels, linked with joints and driven by user parameters.

### Cited Findings
- Assembly files typically contain sub-components Wheels, Main Body, Rear Wing (with rear wheel support structure) and Front Wing. Designs can be "fully constrained using parameters wherever possible for upgradeable designs". - **Wheels:**
  - Teams design lightweight, "aerodynamically efficient" wheels in Fusion, for example with five aerodynamic spokes.
  - Solid 3D-printed PLA wheels have been run with carbon-fibre axles and full-ceramic bearings.
  - Silicon-nitride full-ceramic radial bearings of 7 mm diameter were used, with the hub bore "increased by 0.5 mm to ensure smooth bearing insertion".
  - Hollow, two-layer wheels with an enclosed air section were hard to print, so teams added slanted internal supports forming a cross to the centre hole.

## 4. How teams check the CAD against the Technical Regulations inside Fusion

### Takeaway
Fusion's Inspect tools (Measure, Section Analysis, Interference, physical Properties/mass, Center of Mass, plus curvature and zebra analysis) are the main in-CAD compliance checks, alongside official no-go-zone and cargo bodies modelled as references. Scrutineers use purpose-made gauges and precise measuring tools. Every infringement costs points on each car, and breaking a "critical regulation" makes a team ineligible for the top awards. Most penalties come from ignoring material loss in finishing, and from wing thickness, span and clearance, cartridge-chamber obstructions and weight.

### Inferences
- **Mass estimate:** the block is 223 × 65 × 50 mm = 724.75 cm³ and weighs 112–114 g. That gives a gross density of about **0.155–0.157 g/cm³ (≈155–157 kg/m³)**, ignoring the cartridge hole, which would push the true material density slightly higher. The best practice is to weigh a real block and divide by the official block model's volume from Fusion Properties, then create a custom Fusion physical material with that density. Assign PLA or resin to printed wings and wheels and the correct materials to axles and bearings, then read total mass from Properties at the top-level assembly. Compare this against the class minimum (60 g in Development 2025-26, 80 g in 2026-27) with a margin for paint and sanding.
- A typical in-CAD checklist, derived from the tool list and the rules above:
  - Intersect or Interference the body with the no-go-zone body. Any part of the no-go-zone left outside the body is a violation.
  - Measure the minimum distance from each wing to the body, wheels and track plane (at least 5 mm).
  - Place Section Analysis planes through each wing at several stations to read thickness (2–6 mm in Development).
  - Measure overall length and width against the class limits.
  - Check that the cartridge chamber is unobstructed and that axle and tether-guide bores do not break into it.
  - Check the halo pocket depth and fit against the supplied halo.
  - Use Accessibility Analysis (direction of cut) and Minimum Radius Analysis to confirm the body can be machined with the Denford ball-nose tool.
- Designing a few tenths of a millimetre above each minimum is a sensible hedge against sanding and paint loss, given the Entry guidebook's warning.

## 5. Aerodynamic analysis (CFD and virtual wind tunnels) and how results drive iterations

### Takeaway
Fusion itself has not offered external-aero CFD: a 2019 Autodesk forum answer says it has no fluid simulation. Teams export their geometry to a CFD tool:
- **Ansys Discovery.** Ansys (Synopsys) is now the *exclusive global CFD partner* of STEM Racing. It offers free licences and Innovation Space courses, and a digital-twin racetrack at 2025-26 events.
- **Autodesk CFD.** This was the Autodesk-ecosystem standard, used in coverage of top UK teams around 2023.
- **SimScale** (cloud).
- **Denford/CHAM Virtual Wind Tunnel**, a CFD tool built for the competition.
- **Autodesk Flow Design**, a legacy virtual wind tunnel from the older era.
- Occasionally Solid Edge FloEFD, Caedium or OpenFOAM-style tools.

The usual set-up is a rectangular tunnel around the car, an inlet around 15–22 m/s (most often 20 m/s), a pressure outlet, slip or symmetry walls, and in better set-ups a moving ground and rotating wheels. Teams report drag force, Cd, lift or downforce, and pressure and velocity plots, and iterate component by component.

### Inferences
- In 2025-26 and 2026-27 the official path is Fusion for CAD, then STEP export, then **Ansys Discovery**, backed by the free licences, courses and event demo days. Autodesk CFD remains in use where schools already have it, but it is a separate desktop product, not part of Fusion. Autodesk's own STEM Racing pages now focus on CAD, CAM and rendering.
- Export a watertight STEP solid, not a faceted STL, to avoid the "facets are not well-connected" failure. Remove wheels-to-ground contact slivers, or add a small ground gap or contact patch, before meshing.
- A defensible standard set-up for the portfolio:
  - 20 m/s inlet (a realistic track speed)
  - Tunnel several car lengths long (about 9L), with the car well upstream
  - Pressure outlet at 0 Pa
  - Slip or symmetry side walls and ceiling
  - Moving ground and rotating wheels if the solver allows; optionally a half-car symmetry plane to halve the mesh
  - A mesh-independence check (the same Cd at two mesh densities)
- Report drag force in newtons *and* Cd with the reference (frontal) area stated, because Cd depends on it. A forum thread about measuring projected area for CFD, [F1technical: easy way to measure projected area for CFD?](https://www.f1technical.net/forum/viewtopic.php?t=27430), shows this is a known confusion.
- The iteration pattern teams describe is component-by-component A/B/C testing: nose, front wing, sidepods, rear wing, wheel fairings, with about three variants each (as reported in the Autodesk and DE247 coverage), and a baseline versus final drag delta for the portfolio (for example the ~10% reduction in the academic case). Physical wind-tunnel or smoke-tunnel correlation is a differentiator.

- Autodesk's recent change "Save freely, version intentionally" separates routine saves from deliberate versions. The help page on creating milestones indicates that "Milestones are now called versions" — [Fusion Blog: Toward Smarter Collaboration: Save Freely, Version Intentionally](https://www.autodesk.com/products/fusion-360/blog/smarter-collaboration-autodesk-fusion/); [Fusion Help: Create milestones and versions](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/ENU/Fusion-Import/files/TPD-DESIGN-MS-CREATE.htm). **[Low–Med: the terminology should be checked against the current Fusion release]**
- Fusion branching "operates at a project level": you branch from a point in the whole project, and milestones show the state of each design branch — [Fusion Blog: Branching and Merging in Fusion 360](https://www.autodesk.com/products/fusion-360/blog/branching-and-merging-in-fusion-360/). **[Low as a current feature: this may be legacy or preview]**

### Inferences
- A workable pattern:
  - One shared hub or project per team, with the teacher as admin.
  - Separate design files for the body, each wing and the wheels, inserted into a master assembly as referenced (linked) components, so several students can work in parallel without overwriting each other.
  - Deliberate versions or milestones at each CFD iteration and before each CAM export, with descriptions such as "v7 – nose B, Cd 0.xx". These let a portfolio trace every design change to a version.
- Because scrutineers receive the CAD and judges ask to see it live, the final version must exactly match the physical cars.

## 7. Hand-off: CAM (Fusion Manufacture), 3D printing, engineering drawings, renders and the portfolio

### Takeaway
For CNC, Autodesk's tutorials take the car through Fusion's Manufacture workspace:
1. Prepare the model.
2. Create stock from the block.
3. Orient the model.
4. Pick the cut surfaces.
5. Build toolpaths for both sides.
6. Post NC code.

Teams use a Denford tool library that imports into Fusion and a Denford post-processor. Denford's own QuickCAM Pro, with a two-sided "STEM Racing Car Wizard", is the long-standing alternative. Printed parts (Entry bodies, wings, wheels) leave as mesh exports. Judges require A4 engineering drawings (third-angle orthographic, fully dimensioned with materials, detailed enough for a third party to build the car, proving virtual-cargo compliance) and A4 renders, handed in with the car. The CAD files are also uploaded and demonstrated live.

### Cited Findings
- Autodesk's CAM tutorial "apply[ies] CAM toolpaths and NC-code … to send the car model to a CNC milling machine using Fusion CAM", with step-by-step instructions to "prepare the model to cut, create the stock model, orient the model, pick the surfaces to cut, create CAM toolpath strategies to cut the car, and generate the NC-Code" — [Autodesk Education: STEM Racing](https://www.autodesk.com/education/competitions/f1inschools); [Autodesk Design Academy F1 in Schools curriculum](https://academy.autodesk.com/curriculum/f1-schools-tutorials). **[Med]**
- STEM Racing's downloads include a pre-configured Denford cutting-tool collection for import into Fusion. Denford is the official equipment supplier — [STEM Racing downloads](https://www.stemracing.com/downloads); [STEM Racing: Denford partner page](https://www.stemracing.com/partners/denford). **[Med]**
- Fusion has an online library of free, editable post-processors — [Autodesk Fusion Post Library](https://cam.autodesk.com/hsmposts). On Denford's forum, Fusion 360 is described as able to "communicate with Denford machines", and a custom Fusion post for Denford NovaTurn exists on GitHub — [Denford forum: Fusion 360](https://denfordforum.com/viewtopic.php?t=5570); [Denford forum: Fusion 360 Post Processor](https://www.denfordata.com/bb/viewtopic.php?t=5717). **[Low–Med]**

Australian operator REA publishes QuickCAM guides for both the **F1 Fixture** and **4th-axis** side-machining set-ups, covering model orientation, cut depth, billet size, tool set-up, machining plans and CNC file output — [Denford: QuickCAM Pro](https://denford.co.uk/products/quickcam-pro-software); [REA guide: F1 Fixture – Side Machining](https://learning.rea.org.au/Guide/F1+Fixture+-+Side+Machining+-+QuickCAM+Pro+-+F1+in+Schools+Car+Tutorial/121); [REA guide: 4th Axis – Side Machining](https://learning.rea.org.au/Guide/4th+Axis+-+Side+Machining+-+QuickCAM+Pro+-+F1+in+Schools+Car+Tutorial/119). - Denford sells the Router 2600 PRO with an F1 package that includes QuickCAM Pro, and the Compact 1000 Pro educational router — [TSL: Denford 2600 PRO CNC Router with F1 Package](https://www.technologysupplies.com/cnc-router-2600-pro-with-f1-package-inc-quickcam-pro.html); [Denford: Compact 1000 Pro](https://denford.co.uk/products/router-1000-pro); [Denford F1 in Schools package brochure](https://labmidwest.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/Denford-F1-in-Schools-Package.pdf). **[Med]**
- Entry teams "can produce their car using either a CNC router or a 3D printer" — [Entry Class Competition Guidebook 2025-26](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6913005a89f25e1a2f5bcef5/t/692ec6999b44cc201847e7c6/1764673177837/stem_racing_entry_competition_guidebook_2025_-_2026+(1).pdf). Teams publish print files, for example LEAF1's 2022/23 season car on Printables — [Printables: LEAF1 F1 in Schools 2022/2023](https://www.printables.com/model/381830-leaf1-f1-in-schools-20222023-season-upload). **[Med]**

In Fusion: File → New Drawing → **From Design** opens the Drawing workspace. Each sheet has a title block and border, and title-block fields (title, author, date) are editable. Decal tutorials for Fusion renders: [DECALS – Fusion 360 – F1 in Schools](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1kQNq4tm1LQ). **[Med]**
- **Portfolio and presentation** (2023 World Finals format): a printed 10-page portfolio and a live 10-minute presentation, with points across design, analysis, manufacture, testing and project management — [Autodesk Fusion Blog: Team Nightingale](https://www.autodesk.com/products/fusion-360/blog/team-nightingale-f1-in-schools-world-finals-2023/). **[Med; formats vary by season and class]**
- **Live CAD demonstration:** during Design & Engineering judging, teams need "a computer with the CAD software used by the team and all CAD parts and assembly data available … to demonstrate their CAD work" — [STEM Racing World Finals 2025 Competition Regulations](https://www.stemracing.com/uploads/1/1/8/9/118908723/srwf_2025_competition_regulations_v1.pdf). **[Med]**

### Inferences
- In Fusion CAM, the two-sided block job becomes two setups (top and underside), or side setups for a 4th-axis fixture. Each setup uses stock defined from the official block body, the same WCS datum on the fixture, a roughing pass (adaptive or pocket) with a larger tool, then a finishing pass (parallel or scallop) with the Denford ball-nose tool. The tool library and post come from STEM Racing and Denford downloads. Toolpath simulation should be checked against the no-go-zone, and a small stock-to-leave should be left for sanding. The Accessibility and Minimum Radius analyses from section 4 predict whether the ball-nose can reach every concave area.
- For drawings, an A4 sheet in third-angle projection with front, top and side views, a section through the cartridge chamber and axles, and an isometric view, with a filled-in title block (team number and name, part, material, scale), covers most of the judges' checklist. A dedicated view or dimension set for the virtual cargo (Professional) and halo position is expected.
- Keep the render appearance (decals, paint colours) on a separate "render" version so that visual changes do not create geometry versions that differ from the manufactured cars.

## 8. Educational licensing and learning resources

### Takeaway
Autodesk is STEM Racing's official CAD partner and gives every student and teacher free Fusion through Education licences: one year, renewable while eligible. Learning resources:
- Autodesk's three-level STEM Racing tutorials (beginner, intermediate, advanced), plus CAM.
- The "Fusion Fundamentals" course for teachers.
- Design Academy material, including legacy F1 in Schools courses and the trophy badge guide.
- The Off The Track intermediate series by a World Finals podium designer.
- YouTube series such as "Standard Car", engineering drawing and decals.
- On the CFD side, Ansys Innovation Space courses.
- From Denford, the Virtual Wind Tunnel, QuickCAM Pro and the Fusion tool library.

### Inferences
- Resources split by class:

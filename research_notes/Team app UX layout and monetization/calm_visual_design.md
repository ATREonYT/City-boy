Scope: visual complexity and clutter, hierarchy, density, colour, type, spacing, icons, motion, notifications and badges, dashboards, teenagers, neurodivergent users, empty/loading/error states, light vs dark. Current to September 2026.

## 1. Visual complexity, clutter, first impressions, cognitive load and progressive disclosure: what does the research say, and what thresholds exist?

### Takeaway
People judge how attractive an interface is in well under a second. Those judgements depend mostly on perceived visual complexity and colourfulness, and they carry over into later judgements of usability and trust. Low complexity and a familiar ("prototypical") layout score best. Hard numeric thresholds are rare. The ones that can be defended are ≤5 main choices per screen (W3C COGA), ≤2 levels of progressive disclosure (NN/g), ≤75–80 characters per line (GOV.UK and WCAG), body text of about 16–19 px with line-height of about 1.3–1.5, and "start with the smallest number of statuses" (GOV.UK). None of these limits comes from a controlled test. Most are expert guidance.

### Cited Findings
- [Tested] "Users make lasting judgments about a website's appeal within a split second of seeing it for the first time, and this first impression is influential enough to later affect their opinions of a site's usability and trustworthiness." (Reinecke, Yeh et al., CHI 2013) — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2470654.2481281)
- [Tested] In the same study, 548 volunteers rated 450 websites. Models of perceived visual complexity and colourfulness, combined with demographics such as education and age, explained about half of the variance in appeal ratings given after 500 ms of viewing. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2470654.2481281); [Harvard DASH record](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-cc85-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b)
- [Tested; Secondary summary] Tuch et al. (Google; *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies* 70(11):794–811, 2012): websites with low visual complexity and high prototypicality (looking like a typical site of their kind) got the most favourable first aesthetic judgements, formed within tens of milliseconds. — [Google Research page](https://research.google/pubs/the-role-of-visual-complexity-and-prototypicality-regarding-first-impression-of-websites-working-towards-understanding-aesthetic-judgments/); [DOI 10.1016/j.ijhcs.2012.06.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2012.06.003); summary read in [vitormattos.github.io design review](https://github.com/vitormattos/vitormattos.github.io) (docs/portfolio-design-review.md)
- [Tested; citation only] Lindgaard, Fernandes, Dudek & Brown (2006), "Attention web designers: You have 50 milliseconds to make a good first impression!", *Behaviour & Information Technology* 25(2):115–126. The citation is confirmed. The 50 ms claim is the paper's headline finding, but I did not access the study details. — [ACM TOCHI reference list reproduction](https://github.com/vintageplayer/SciBase-ACMTemp)
- [Guideline; Secondary] NN/g, progressive disclosure: "Designs that go beyond 2 disclosure levels typically have low usability because users often get lost when moving between the levels." The principle is to show the most important options first and defer advanced or rarely used ones to an explicit request. — [NN/g, Progressive Disclosure](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/), quoted verbatim in [secustor/renovate-config-debugger research note](https://github.com/secustor/renovate-config-debugger) and [DIGI-UW/OpenELIS-Global-2 research note](https://github.com/DIGI-UW/OpenELIS-Global-2)
- [Guideline] W3C COGA pattern "Avoid Too Much Content": "Keep the interface simple. Provide users with five or less main choices on each screen and remove unnecessary content." Unrelated links go in footers, and extra options go behind "more" links with descriptive labels. It also recommends limiting a screen to one or two related images and using short bullets instead of long paragraphs. Rationale: too much content causes cognitive overload, anxiety and loss of focus, which hits slow readers and people with attention difficulties hardest. — [w3c/coga design-guide/o5p03-manageable-quantity.html](https://github.com/w3c/coga/blob/main/design-guide/o5p03-manageable-quantity.html); [COGA "Making Content Usable" (W3C Note)](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)
- [Guideline] COGA "use" example: "A simple interface. The main feature is much bigger than anything else." "Avoid" example: "Too much text, long menus, and images set around long paragraphs of dense text." — [w3c/coga o5p03](https://github.com/w3c/coga/blob/main/design-guide/o5p03-manageable-quantity.html)
- [Guideline] COGA "Support Simplification": let users "Remove or hide features that most users do not use or that are not essential", and offer a simplified version. The recommended example is an app with "3 large features" and everything else "in the footer or under a 'more' option". — [w3c/coga design-guide/o8p03-complexity.html](https://github.com/w3c/coga/blob/main/design-guide/o8p03-complexity.html)
- [Guideline] GOV.UK Tag: "The more you add, the harder it is for users to remember them. So start with the smallest number of statuses you think might work, then add more if your user research shows there's a need for them." — [GOV.UK Design System, Tag (source)](https://github.com/alphagov/govuk-design-system/blob/main/src/components/tag/index.md)
- [Guideline] IBM Carbon status indicators: "While we won't set a strict limit, having more than five or six indicators can overwhelm users." Where no action is required, "use plain text to prevent overloading the interface with unnecessary indicators." — [Carbon, Status indicator pattern (source)](https://github.com/carbon-design-system/carbon-website/blob/d8783ad2ae3b5e59c58f58311491f8a2c4e62631/src/pages/patterns/status-indicator-pattern/index.mdx)
- [Guideline] Carbon dashboards: "Design a dashboard to reduce a page's complexity by stripping away anything that could distract a user from interpreting the information." Carbon also says "white space increases comprehension by almost 20 percent", citing Human Factors International. It does not name the original study or its conditions, so treat that figure with caution. — [Carbon, Dashboards (source)](https://github.com/carbon-design-system/carbon-website/blob/d8783ad2ae3b5e59c58f58311491f8a2c4e62631/src/pages/data-visualization/dashboards/index.mdx)
- [Industry research; not peer-reviewed; Secondary] Google's Material 3 Expressive research (2025; 46 studies, 18,000+ participants) reports that participants found key UI elements "up to four times faster" on expressive screens, which use size, shape, containment and colour on purpose. Older adults (45+) performed at parity with younger users. — quoted in [breezy-weather HELP.md](https://github.com/breezy-weather/breezy-weather/blob/d587328ae9755c8801f8cbc252a16ead7ebaea5a/HELP.md) and [LearnedGeek UI_UX_Guidelines.md](https://github.com/LearnedGeek/ApiCombatGame/blob/95b7c7bca72e7f5fbe5129c04d10eca6ee5c5d60/docs/UI_UX_Guidelines.md)

### Inferences
- "Calm" does not mean "bland". The evidence favours low overall complexity, a familiar structure, and one element that is clearly dominant. Google's Expressive data and COGA's "main feature much bigger than anything else" both say salience should be concentrated, not removed.
- Prototypicality matters (Tuch). SubOne's conventional left sidebar, header and content layout is an asset. Novel navigation would cost first-impression appeal.
- Most "magic numbers" (5 choices, 2 levels, 75 characters) are expert guidance, not tested cut-offs. Use them as defaults to test against, not as proven thresholds.
- Implication for SubOne: Treat the first view of every section as a "500 ms test": one dominant element (the section's main object or next action), a familiar frame, and nothing decorative competing with it.
- Implication for SubOne: Cap first-level choices at about 5 per screen. For example, Plan's six views (calendar/timeline/board/workload/risks/playbook) could show 3–4 as tabs, put the rest under a labelled "More views", and remember each team's default.
- Implication for SubOne: Keep disclosure to two levels (section → item detail in a drawer or page). Avoid drawers inside drawers and accordions inside tabs inside panels.
- Implication for SubOne: Limit prose areas (Playbook, Portfolio, AI answers, rules-check explanations) to about 75 characters per line. Never let paragraphs span the full width on a laptop.
- Implication for SubOne: Keep body text at 16 px or more on phones and laptops, with line-height of about 1.4–1.5 for paragraphs. Do not shrink body text on mobile (GOV.UK keeps 19 px). Keep secondary labels at 12–13 px or more (inference). Tiny text fails with teens (see Q6).
- Implication for SubOne: Keep the status vocabulary per object small (for example 3–4 states for a part or a task), and add states only when research shows a need.
- Implication for SubOne: Test every screen with WCAG 1.4.12 spacing overrides, which dyslexic users apply through browser extensions, so nothing clips or overlaps.

- I could not access Baymard's line-length research. The commonly cited 50–75 characters could not be verified, so the GOV.UK and WCAG figures above are used instead.
- Reinecke & Gajos (CHI 2014, "Quantifying visual preferences around the world") on how preferences for colourfulness and complexity vary by age, education and country: not accessed.
- Exact exposure times and effect sizes for Tuch et al. and Lindgaard et al.: not verified. Numbers seen in some secondary sources (for example correlation values) were unreliable and are omitted.
- I found no validated maximum number of colours per screen and no tested limit on the number of cards.
- Hick's law, Miller's 7±2 and Cowan's 4±1 were not verified in this session. They are often misapplied to menu length, so do not cite them as thresholds without checking.
- NN/g's "Minimize Cognitive Load" and visual-hierarchy articles (including any "no more than 3 type sizes" rule of thumb) could not be accessed.
- I found no verified source for a 4/8 px spacing grid in this session. Carbon's row heights (multiples of 8) and Material's 4 px density step are consistent with one.

## 2. How have leading products and design systems reduced visual noise (Linear, Material 3, Fluent 2, Carbon, GOV.UK, Apple, Notion, Things)?

### Takeaway
The clearest recent example is Linear's March 2026 refresh, published as "A calmer interface for a product in motion". It dimmed the navigation so content wins, cut the number and size of icons, removed coloured icon backgrounds, made headers and view controls consistent everywhere, and moved to a warmer, less saturated grey. Its 2024 system builds every theme from three variables (base, accent, contrast) in LCH, which gives a high-contrast theme for free. The design systems add the same kind of restraint: optional density (Material, Carbon), "productive" motion by default (Carbon), a small set of semantic status colours always paired with shape or text (Carbon, GOV.UK), and dark-grey rather than black dark themes (Material).

### Cited Findings
- [Practice] Linear, "A calmer interface for a product in motion" (Charlie Aufmann & Maxime Heckel, 12 March 2026). The aim is "a calmer, more consistent interface" that makes it "easier to scan information, navigate between workflows, and stay focused." — [Linear blog](https://linear.app/now/behind-the-latest-design-refresh); [Linear changelog: UI refresh](https://linear.app/changelog/2026-03-12-ui-refresh)
- [Practice] Linear 2026: "Headers, navigation, and view controls are now consistent across projects, issues, reviews, and documents; icons have been redrawn and resized." The navigation sidebar is "a few notches dimmer, allowing the main content area—where users work—to take precedence." — [Linear blog](https://linear.app/now/behind-the-latest-design-refresh)
- [Practice] Linear 2026: the refresh "reduces icon usage, scales their sizes down, and removes unnecessary visual treatments like colored team icon backgrounds." — [Linear blog](https://linear.app/now/behind-the-latest-design-refresh)
- [Practice] Linear 2026 colour: "The old palette was a cool, blue-ish hue, and the aim was to inch toward a warmer gray that still feels crisp, but less saturated. Go too warm, though, and the interface risks looking muddy." The team built a colour tool in its dev toolbar (made with Claude Code) to tune the hue, chroma and lightness of individual design tokens. — [Linear blog](https://linear.app/now/behind-the-latest-design-refresh)
- [Practice] Linear 2024 redesign (shipped 20 March 2024; written up in "How we redesigned the Linear UI (part II)"): themes are defined by three variables (base colour, accent colour, contrast) instead of 98 hand-set variables, and more than a hundred colour variables are derived from them in LCH. LCH was chosen because it is close to human perception and handles surface elevations well. The contrast variable automatically produces "super high-contrast themes for users who need it for accessibility reasons." — [Linear blog, part II](https://linear.app/now/how-we-redesigned-the-linear-ui); [Linear changelog, 2024-03-20](https://linear.app/changelog/2024-03-20-new-linear-ui)
- [Practice; titles only verified] Linear shipped a "Personalized sidebar" (18 Dec 2024) and "Customize your navigation in Linear Mobile" (22 Jan 2026). — [Linear changelog 2024-12-18](https://linear.app/changelog/2024-12-18-personalized-sidebar); [Linear changelog 2026-01-22](https://linear.app/changelog/2026-01-22-customize-your-navigation-in-linear-mobile)
- [Guideline] Material density: "the density system only allows negative numbers... The lower the density scale, the higher the component density." Each step changes height by 4 px (a 36 px button becomes 24 px at −3). Dense layouts help with "lists, tables, and long forms". "Touch targets are automatically disabled when density mixins are applied, since dense components should be optionally enabled." — [Material Components Web, density README](https://github.com/material-components/material-components-web/blob/master/packages/mdc-density/README.md)
- [Guideline] Carbon data-table row sizes: xs 24 px, sm 32, md 40, lg 48, xl 64. "Extra large row heights are only recommended if your data is expected to have two lines of content in a single row." — [Carbon data table style (source)](https://github.com/carbon-design-system/carbon-website/blob/d8783ad2ae3b5e59c58f58311491f8a2c4e62631/src/pages/components/data-table/style.mdx)
- [Guideline] Material dark theme: "background and surface colors are dark grey instead of black, which increases visibility for shadows and also reduces eye strain for light text". In dark mode, elevation is shown by surfaces becoming "lighter and more colorful at higher elevations". — [Material Components Android, Dark theme docs](https://github.com/material-components/material-components-android/blob/master/docs/theming/Dark.md)

"Expressive motion" is for "significant moments such as opening a new page, clicking the primary action button, or when the movement itself conveys a meaning." Tokens: 70/110/150/240/400/700 ms. — [Carbon motion overview (source)](https://github.com/carbon-design-system/carbon-website/blob/d8783ad2ae3b5e59c58f58311491f8a2c4e62631/src/pages/elements/motion/overview.mdx)

- [Guideline] GOV.UK: "Do not use colour alone to convey information"; keep a tag's colour consistent across instances. Use notification banners "sparingly. There's evidence that people often miss them, and using them too often is likely to make this problem worse." — [GOV.UK Tag (source)](https://github.com/alphagov/govuk-design-system/blob/main/src/components/tag/index.md); [GOV.UK notification banner (source)](https://github.com/alphagov/govuk-design-system/blob/main/src/components/notification-banner/index.md)
- [Platform practice] Apple tiers notification urgency into four interruption levels: passive, active, time-sensitive and critical (details in Q5). — [Apple UNNotificationInterruptionLevel](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/usernotifications/unnotificationinterruptionlevel) (text read via [tmc/apple mirror](https://github.com/tmc/apple/blob/872b4b8c75cb24617d606e0484c1db1e2948f01e/usernotifications/enums.gen.go))

### Inferences
- The common pattern is that chrome recedes while content and exceptions stand out. Colour is kept for meaning (status, selection, the brand mark). Icons are fewer, smaller and monochrome. Density is optional. Motion is productive by default.
- SubOne's direction is already close to this (near-black ink, team colour only on marks, green/amber/red for status). The main gaps are likely sidebar contrast, icon count and weight, consistent section headers, status-colour discipline, and the lack of density, contrast and focus options.
- Implication for SubOne: Make the left sidebar a few steps dimmer than the content: mid-grey labels and icons, only the current item in near-black, and no filled coloured blocks.
- Implication for SubOne: Use icons in the sidebar only, one size and one ink colour, with no coloured tiles. Keep the team colour on the team mark and perhaps a thin current-item indicator.
- Implication for SubOne: Use one header anatomy on every section (title, primary action, view switcher, filters in the same places) so Plan, Car, Race and Money feel like one product (Linear 2026).
- Implication for SubOne: Build colour tokens from base, accent (team colour) and contrast variables in a perceptual space (LCH/OKLCH). That gives light, dark and high-contrast themes without extra hand-tuning.
- Implication for SubOne: Use a slightly warm, low-chroma grey ramp for surfaces and borders. Avoid cool, blue-tinted greys that fight the team colour, and avoid greys so warm they look muddy.
- Implication for SubOne: Offer "Comfortable" density (default, touch-safe) and "Compact" (laptop only) for tables and boards (Plan board and workload, Race times, Money ledger), changing by about 4–8 px per row.
- Implication for SubOne: Let users pin, reorder and hide sidebar sections (Linear's personalised sidebar; COGA "Support Simplification"), so a driver can hide Money and a finance lead can hide Aero.
- Implication for SubOne: Adopt one small motion scale (for example 100/150/200/300 ms with M3 standard easing) and one status palette in which only exceptions get strong colour.

- Notion and Things 3 (Cultured Code): no design documentation was accessed in this session, so no verified claims.
- Apple HIG (including the 2025 "Liquid Glass" hierarchy changes and any legibility criticism) and Microsoft Fluent 2's written principles and colour guidance: blocked or not accessed. Only Fluent tokens and Apple's notification API text were verified.
- Material 3 colour-role and badge guidance: m3.material.io was blocked.
- Linear's own density settings (if any): not verified.

## 3. Dashboards and "home"/Today screens: how many cards or metrics before overload, how to foreground one next action, and how to design status colours, charts and numbers for glanceability

### Takeaway
A dashboard exists to show "the most important information needed to achieve one or more objectives", on "a single screen" and "at a glance" (Few). Carbon's guidance is to order by importance, put the most critical item at the top, strip distractions, use consistent colours per data set, express KPIs as "a number with a single word description", and never rely on colour alone. More than 5–6 status indicators overwhelm users. I found no empirical card limit. The best proxies are COGA's ≤5 main choices, Carbon's 5–6 indicators, and Few's single-screen rule.

### Cited Findings
- [Guideline; Secondary, verified against the book by the reproducer] Stephen Few: "A dashboard is a visual display of the most important information needed to achieve one or more objectives; consolidated and arranged on a single screen so the information can be monitored at a glance." And: "If you must scroll around to see all the information, it has transgressed the boundaries of a dashboard." And: "more science than art, more simplicity than dazzle." (*Information Dashboard Design*, 2006; 2nd ed. 2013) — [borg-collective Few source notes](https://github.com/noah-goodrich/borg-collective/blob/9738fd86895746d5e7430235f5ff9d5126cc75dd/docs/infoviz/research/sources/operational-few-dashboard-design.md)
- [Guideline; Secondary] The first four of Few's 13 dashboard pitfalls: exceeding the boundaries of a single screen; supplying inadequate context for the data (a number with no target, comparison or trend); displaying excessive detail or precision; and expressing measures indirectly. — [analytics-design-studio pitfalls list](https://github.com/duda-creator/analytics-design-studio/blob/3f04179700801181ddca389abe3c5dbce54cd002/references/Dashboard_Visual_Design_Pitfalls.md); original [Perceptual Edge white paper](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf) (not fetched)
- [Guideline] Carbon distinguishes "presentation" dashboards (current KPI status, a big-picture view that guides where to focus) from "exploration" dashboards (search, sort, filter, drill-down). For presentation dashboards: "prioritize data by importance, then create a clear visual hierarchy". Put the most critical information at the top, then "follow the F-pattern for the remaining elements, finishing with the least important information." "Always use consistent colors for each data set within a dashboard." For exploration dashboards: "All charts should use the same layout and spacing, and have legends in the same position." — [Carbon Dashboards (source)](https://github.com/carbon-design-system/carbon-website/blob/d8783ad2ae3b5e59c58f58311491f8a2c4e62631/src/pages/data-visualization/dashboards/index.mdx)
- [Guideline] Carbon KPI anatomy: "A key performance indicator (KPI) consists of a number with a single word description. Examples include '15,250 browsers', '$1.3M revenue'." Place the legend at the bottom or top "where space is scarce, such as a dashboard." — [Carbon chart anatomy (source)](https://github.com/carbon-design-system/carbon-website/blob/d8783ad2ae3b5e59c58f58311491f8a2c4e62631/src/pages/data-visualization/chart-anatomy/index.mdx); [Carbon legends (source)](https://github.com/carbon-design-system/carbon-website/blob/d8783ad2ae3b5e59c58f58311491f8a2c4e62631/src/pages/data-visualization/legends/index.mdx)
- [Guideline] Carbon status attention levels. High attention (immediate action): alerts, exceptions, errors. Medium: progress and acknowledgements. Low (passive): items ready to view, change indicators. "Avoid using status indicators when no user action is required or when the status information isn't significant enough to highlight." "More than five or six indicators can overwhelm users." — [Carbon status indicators (source)](https://github.com/carbon-design-system/carbon-website/blob/d8783ad2ae3b5e59c58f58311491f8a2c4e62631/src/pages/patterns/status-indicator-pattern/index.mdx)
- [Standard] WCAG 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast (AA): the visual parts of UI components and graphical objects needed to understand content need "a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against adjacent color(s)". This covers status dots, chart lines and bars, and input borders. — [WCAG 1.4.11 (source)](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/guidelines/sc/21/non-text-contrast.html)
- [Guideline] GOV.UK: do not use colour alone; keep colour consistent for the same tag. — [GOV.UK Tag (source)](https://github.com/alphagov/govuk-design-system/blob/main/src/components/tag/index.md)
- [Guideline] COGA: "The main feature is much bigger than anything else"; ≤5 main choices per screen. — [w3c/coga o5p03](https://github.com/w3c/coga/blob/main/design-guide/o5p03-manageable-quantity.html)
- [Industry research; Secondary] Using size and containment to make key elements salient helped users find them up to 4× faster (Google M3 Expressive research). — [breezy-weather HELP.md quoting Google](https://github.com/breezy-weather/breezy-weather/blob/d587328ae9755c8801f8cbc252a16ead7ebaea5a/HELP.md)

### Inferences
- A "calm" Today screen is a presentation dashboard. It answers "what should I do now, and is anything wrong?" at a glance. Exploration (race statistics, money analysis, workload charts) belongs in the sections, not on Today.
- "Colour the exceptions, not the norm" follows from Carbon's advice to use plain text when no action is needed and from GOV.UK's "smallest number of statuses". When everything is green, green becomes noise.
- Implication for SubOne: Today should fit one laptop screen without scrolling. Start with one dominant "Next up for you" card at top-left (the single next action), then at most 4 supporting cards. That keeps Today at 5 or fewer primary items (COGA, Carbon).
- Implication for SubOne: Show each stat on Today as a number plus a short label plus one line of context (target, deadline or change since last week). Few's "inadequate context" pitfall rules out bare numbers.
- Implication for SubOne: Round and trim precision on Today (for example "£1.2k left", "best lap 5.21 s"). Keep full precision in Race and Money.
- Implication for SubOne: Show "on track" as plain text or neutral grey. Use amber only for "at risk, needs a decision" and red only for "blocked or overdue, needs you". Always pair colour with an icon and a word, and give status marks ≥3:1 contrast.
- Implication for SubOne: Give each data series (driver, car version, budget category) one fixed colour across all Race and Money charts. Keep legend position and chart spacing the same everywhere. Mute the team colour in charts or use it only for "our team" against competitors.
- Implication for SubOne: On phones, collapse Today into one column in the same priority order: next action, then exceptions, then context.

### Gaps
- No empirical study was found that fixes a maximum number of dashboard cards or KPIs. Figures such as "5–9 KPIs" seen in industry blogs are expert opinion and were not verified.
- Few's specific colour guidance (muted colours for most data, bright colour only for highlighting) and the remaining 9 of the 13 pitfalls: not verified in this session.
- NN/g's dashboard and preattentive-attributes articles, Material 3 and Fluent 2 data-visualisation guidance, and glanceability research: not accessed.

## 4. Motion: when animation helps orientation and when it overstimulates; durations, easing, prefers-reduced-motion and WCAG 2.2

### Takeaway
Most interface motion should last about 100–300 ms, and almost never more than 400–500 ms. Exits should be slightly faster than entries, and duration should scale with distance travelled. Material, Fluent and Carbon token scales all cluster there, and Carbon calls this "productive" motion. Save "expressive" motion for rare, meaningful moments. WCAG requires a pause, stop or hide control for anything that moves, blinks or auto-updates for more than 5 s alongside other content (Level A). WCAG also recommends that motion triggered by interaction can be switched off (AAA), which in practice means honouring `prefers-reduced-motion`. Motion distracts people with attention difficulties and can cause dizziness and nausea in people with vestibular disorders.

### Cited Findings
- [Guideline; Secondary] NN/g, "Executing UX Animations: Duration and Motion Characteristics": "the duration of most animations should be in the range of 100–500 ms". Simple feedback (checkbox, toggle) takes about 100 ms. Substantial screen changes such as a modal take 200–300 ms. The practical range is 100–400 ms. Reserve ~400 ms for large movements across spacious screens. At ~500 ms animations feel like "a real drag". Entrances should run slightly longer than exits (for example 300 ms in, 200–250 ms out), and duration should grow with distance. — [NN/g animation duration](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-duration/), numbers read from [a primary-sourced reproduction](https://github.com/kengomatsuo/product-video/blob/4dfcc4d91e76108848142ceb4f080d0afa34b163/skills/product-video/research/timing-sources/nngroup-animation-duration.md)
- [Guideline] Token scales: Material 3 runs 50–1000 ms (short 50–200, medium 250–400, long 450–600, extra-long 700–1000) with standard easing cubic-bezier(0.2, 0, 0, 1). Fluent 2 runs 50–500 ms. Carbon runs 70–700 ms. — [flutter motion.dart](https://github.com/flutter/flutter/blob/master/packages/flutter/lib/src/material/motion.dart); [fluentui durations.ts](https://github.com/microsoft/fluentui/blob/master/packages/tokens/src/global/durations.ts); [Carbon motion (source)](https://github.com/carbon-design-system/carbon-website/blob/d8783ad2ae3b5e59c58f58311491f8a2c4e62631/src/pages/elements/motion/overview.mdx)
- [Guideline] Carbon: productive motion is "subtle and out of the way" for task focus, and expressive motion is for "significant moments". "Motion's duration should be dynamic based on the size of the animation; the larger the change in distance (traveled) or size (scaling)... the longer the animation takes." "Always provide alternatives for interface state transitions. Consider simplified or reduced motion designs for mobile and tablet. Make sure there is always a way to communicate similar messages statically." — [Carbon motion (source)](https://github.com/carbon-design-system/carbon-website/blob/d8783ad2ae3b5e59c58f58311491f8a2c4e62631/src/pages/elements/motion/overview.mdx)
- [Standard] WCAG 2.2 SC 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide (Level A). Moving, blinking or scrolling content that starts automatically, lasts more than 5 seconds and is shown alongside other content needs a way to pause, stop or hide it. Auto-updating content needs pause, stop, hide or control over update frequency. The Understanding document says 5 s "is long enough to get a user's attention, but not so long that a user cannot wait out the distraction". It also says "There is no five second exception for auto-updating", that "those with attention deficit disorders find blinking content distracting", and that "Moving content can also be a severe distraction for some people." — [WCAG Understanding 2.2.2 (source)](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/understanding/20/pause-stop-hide.html); [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)
- [Standard] WCAG 2.2 SC 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions (Level AAA): motion animation triggered by interaction can be disabled unless it is essential. The intent is "to allow users to prevent animation from being displayed on web pages". People with vestibular disorders can experience "dizziness, nausea and headaches", sometimes severe enough to need "bed rest to recover". Movement that the user directly controls, such as scrolling, is allowed. The document points to `prefers-reduced-motion` and user controls. — [WCAG Understanding 2.3.3 (source)](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/understanding/21/animation-from-interactions.html)
- [Standard] WCAG 2.2 SC 2.3.1 Three Flashes or Below Threshold (Level A): nothing may flash more than three times in any one-second period, unless the flash is below the general and red flash thresholds. — [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) (criterion text not re-fetched this session)
- [Guideline] MDN: "Blinking and flashing animation can be problematic for people with cognitive concerns such as Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD). Additionally, certain kinds of motion can be a trigger for Vestibular disorders, epilepsy, and migraine, and Scotopic sensitivity." Provide "a mechanism for pausing or disabling animation, as well as using the Reduced Motion Media Query". — [MDN Web Animations API (source)](https://github.com/mdn/content/blob/main/files/en-us/web/api/web_animations_api/index.md); [MDN prefers-reduced-motion (source)](https://github.com/mdn/content/blob/main/files/en-us/web/css/reference/at-rules/@media/prefers-reduced-motion/index.md)
- [Guideline] COGA "Support Adaptation and Personalization" includes a motion pattern (o8p01-motion), and "Limit Interruptions" names moving content and changes the user did not start as barriers to focus. — [w3c/coga content-usable index](https://github.com/w3c/coga/blob/main/content-usable/index.html); [COGA Limit Interruptions](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG2/supplemental/patterns/o5p01-minimal-interruptions/)

### Inferences
- Motion helps when it explains a change of state or place: where a panel came from, what just saved, what moved. It overstimulates when it is decorative, looping, large, slow or unrequested.
- Implication for SubOne: Use 100–150 ms for hover, press and toggle feedback, 200–250 ms for drawers, menus and toasts, and at most 300–400 ms for full-view transitions. Exits should be faster than entries, with standard easing and no bounce or overshoot.
- Implication for SubOne: Nothing should loop or auto-play. The Car section's 3D viewer should not auto-rotate by default, and any auto-motion over 5 s needs a visible pause (WCAG 2.2.2).
- Implication for SubOne: Live race timing that auto-updates needs a "pause live updates" or update-frequency control (WCAG 2.2.2 has no 5-second exception for auto-updating), and new values should highlight briefly instead of flashing.
- Implication for SubOne: Honour `prefers-reduced-motion` and add an in-app "Reduce motion" switch in Settings. Replace slides and zooms with short fades or instant changes, and turn off 3D inertia and spin.
- Implication for SubOne: Keep expressive motion (confetti-like celebration) for rare real milestones, such as the car passing the rules check or a submission being sent. Never use it to reward repeat engagement (see ICO in Q6).

### Gaps
- The share of adults with vestibular dysfunction (often quoted as about 35% of US adults aged 40+) could not be verified in this session.
- Research on animated transitions helping orientation (for example studies of animated transitions in data visualisation) was not accessed. The "helps orientation" claim above rests on design-system guidance, not verified studies.
- Apple HIG motion guidance was not accessed.

## 5. Notifications, badges and red dots: evidence on anxiety and interruption cost; batching, digests and quiet hours; calm-technology principles

### Takeaway
Interruptions are expensive. In Gloria Mark's field observations, interrupted work took on average about 23 minutes to resume, and in her lab work people compensated by working faster under more stress. Batching notifications three times a day improved attention, productivity, mood and sense of control compared with the usual flow. Turning notifications off entirely brought few of those benefits and raised anxiety and FoMO. Teens already get a median of 237 notifications a day, about a quarter of them during school. The calm pattern is tiered urgency (Apple's passive, active, time-sensitive and critical levels), predictable digests, user control over channels, quiet hours, and indicators only when action is needed. I found no rigorous study specifically on badge or "red dot" anxiety.

### Cited Findings
- [Field study; Secondary] Gloria Mark's field observations (paraphrase): about 82% (81.9%) of interrupted work was resumed the same day, on average after 23 minutes 15 seconds, and people typically did about two other tasks before returning, so reorienting took extra effort. — [Gallup interview with Gloria Mark](https://news.gallup.com/businessjournal/23146/too-many-interruptions-work.aspx). The exact wording was not verified, because I read these numbers in search summaries of the interview and derivative pages. The figure is often attributed to Mark, Gudith & Klocke (2008), but it comes from Mark's earlier field research as reported in this interview, and its provenance has been examined critically ([oberien, "Interruptions cost 23 minutes 15 seconds, right?"](https://blog.oberien.de/2023/11/05/23-minutes-15-seconds.html)).
- [Tested; title-level] Mark, Gudith & Klocke (CHI 2008), "The cost of interrupted work: More speed and stress". The headline finding is that interrupted people work faster but at the cost of more stress. — [summary page](https://www.highperformanceroutines.com/the-cost-of-interrupted-work-more-speed-and-stress/)
- [Tested] Fitz, Kushlev et al. (2019, *Computers in Human Behavior*) ran a randomised field experiment with N = 237. Compared with the control condition, people whose notifications were batched three times a day "felt more attentive, productive, in a better mood, and in greater control of their phones", and also reported lower stress and fewer interruptions. People who received no notifications at all got few of these benefits and had higher anxiety and "fear of missing out". — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563219302596); [APA PsycNet](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2019-56597-011)
- [Tested; title-level] Kushlev, Proulx & Dunn (CHI 2016), "'Silence your phones': Smartphone notifications increase inattention and hyperactivity symptoms" (pp. 1011–1020). Note that one secondary citation omits Proulx. — [DOI 10.1145/2858036.2858359](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858359)
- [Survey] Common Sense Media, *Constant Companion* (2023): 203 US young people aged 11–17 were tracked for a week with a passive-sensing app. They received a median of 237 notifications a day; "about a quarter arrived during the school day, and 5% at night". They used their phones for about 43 minutes during school hours (a search summary called this an average; the report may give it as a median, which I did not verify). More than two-thirds said they "sometimes" or "often" find it hard to stop using technology. — [Common Sense press release](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/press-releases/teens-are-bombarded-with-hundreds-of-notifications-a-day); [report PDF](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/sites/default/files/research/report/2023-cs-smartphone-research-report_final-for-web.pdf); [CNN coverage](https://www.cnn.com/2023/09/26/health/teen-hundreds-of-phone-notifications-report-wellness)
- [Platform practice] Apple interruption levels, quoted from Apple's documentation. Passive: "The system adds the notification to the notification list without lighting up the screen or playing a sound." Active: "presents the notification immediately, lights up the screen, and can play a sound." Time-sensitive: also "breaks through system notification controls." Critical: "bypasses the mute switch to play a sound." — [Apple docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/usernotifications/unnotificationinterruptionlevel) via [tmc/apple mirror](https://github.com/tmc/apple/blob/872b4b8c75cb24617d606e0484c1db1e2948f01e/usernotifications/enums.gen.go)
- [Guideline] W3C COGA "Limit Interruptions": provide "an easy way to control interruptions, reminders, and changes in content unless they are started by the user or involve an emergency". Let users "control and limit types of content which could cause them distraction". The recommended example is an app that "lets the user decide how they want to be notified about reminders and emails. Users can choose visual reminders, sounds, or none." Avoid pop-ups that must be closed before continuing, and offer "an easy to use pause option so interruptions and moving content can be viewed later." — [COGA Limit Interruptions](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG2/supplemental/patterns/o5p01-minimal-interruptions/); [source](https://github.com/w3c/coga/blob/main/design-guide/o5p01-minimal-interruptions.html)
- [Guideline] GOV.UK notification banners: "Use notification banners sparingly. There's evidence that people often miss them, and using them too often is likely to make this problem worse." "Avoid showing more than one notification banner on the same page. Instead, combine the messages." — [GOV.UK notification banner (source)](https://github.com/alphagov/govuk-design-system/blob/main/src/components/notification-banner/index.md)
- [Guideline] Carbon: separate high, medium and low attention levels, and do not show indicators when no action is needed. — [Carbon status indicators (source)](https://github.com/carbon-design-system/carbon-website/blob/d8783ad2ae3b5e59c58f58311491f8a2c4e62631/src/pages/patterns/status-indicator-pattern/index.mdx)
- [Expert opinion] Amber Case's Calm Technology principles, building on Weiser & Brown: (1) require the smallest possible amount of attention; (2) inform and create calm; (3) make use of the periphery; (4) amplify the best of technology and of humanity; (5) communicate, but don't need to speak; (6) work even when it fails; (7) the right amount of technology is the minimum needed to solve the problem; (8) respect social norms. — [Amber Case, Principles of Calm Technology](https://caseorganic.com/post/principles-of-calm-technology/); [Calm Tech Institute principles](https://www.calmtech.institute/calm-tech-principles); [Wikipedia: Calm technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calm_technology)

### Inferences
- The evidence supports predictable, batched delivery with user control over channels, not zero notifications. Silence has its own cost (anxiety and FoMO in Fitz et al.).
- Badges are ambient notifications. The same logic applies: counts should mean "things that need you", and anything else should be a quiet "new" dot or nothing at all.
- Implication for SubOne: Adopt four levels modelled on Apple's. Passive: inbox only, no push, no count (team activity, comments on things you follow). Active: push or email (direct mentions, tasks assigned to you). Time-sensitive: deadlines today and race-day logistics. Critical: none by default.
- Implication for SubOne: Make digests the default for non-urgent items (for example a morning summary and an after-school summary, in line with Fitz's three-a-day batching). Let users choose instant, digest or off per category.
- Implication for SubOne: For student accounts, set default quiet hours during the school day and at night. Common Sense found about 25% of teen notifications arrive during school and 5% at night. Time-sensitive race-day items can break through only when a teacher or mentor enables race mode (inference).
- Implication for SubOne: Show numeric badges only for items the person must act on (assigned tasks, direct mentions, approvals). Use a small neutral dot for "new", avoid red counts for anything non-urgent, cap counts at "9+", and never put counts on every sidebar item.
- Implication for SubOne: The AI assistant should never interrupt. It is user-invoked, and any suggestions sit quietly in the periphery (a hint line or a side panel), following Calm Tech principles 3 and 5 and COGA's pop-up guidance.
- Implication for SubOne: Show at most one in-app banner per page, and use banners rarely (GOV.UK). Route routine confirmations to short, auto-dismissing, non-blocking toasts or to inline status.
- Implication for SubOne: In Chat, show unread counts only for DMs and mentions. Channel activity gets a bold channel name or dot, not a number, and a per-channel mute should be one click away.

### Gaps
- Badge and "red dot" anxiety: I found no accessible peer-reviewed study that isolates notification badges. Claims that red badges cause anxiety should be treated as expert opinion unless the report writer finds a study.
- Stothart, Mitchum & Yehnert (2015), "The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification" (*JEP: Human Perception & Performance* 41(4):893), and Pielot & Rello (2017), "Productive, anxious, lonely – 24 hours without push notifications" (MobileHCI '17): citations confirmed via the reference list of [arXiv 1705.06224](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.06224) as reproduced in [allenai/PeerRead](https://github.com/allenai/PeerRead), but I could not read their findings.
- Iqbal & Bailey's work on deferring notifications to task breakpoints (CHI 2008) was not accessed.
- Gloria Mark's "47-second average attention on a screen" (from *Attention Span*, 2023) was not verified. It is widely repeated, and at least one research critique calls it poorly sourced.
- Common Sense 2023 phone-checking frequency: a search summary said "over 100 times per day", but I could not confirm this against the report, so it is omitted.

## 6. Teenagers (and young adults): what they like and dislike in interfaces; UK ICO Children's Code on layout and nudges

### Takeaway
NN/g's teen studies (100 teens aged 13–17 over three rounds) found that teens appreciate good aesthetics but "detest" clutter and pointless multimedia. They dislike tiny text as much as adults do. They move fast, read less well and are more impatient than adults, and they often use touch devices where small, precise targets fail. The UK Children's Code applies to services likely to be used by under-18s. It requires the child's best interests, high-privacy defaults, age-appropriate transparency, and no nudges that lead children to share more data or weaken privacy. Secondary summaries also describe it as challenging engagement-extending features such as reward loops and auto-play.

### Cited Findings
- [Tested] NN/g teen research: 100 users aged 13–17 across three study rounds (38, 46 and 16 teens), producing 130 usability guidelines. — [NN/g, Teenager's UX](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-of-websites-for-teenagers/); [NN/g report, UX Design for Teenagers](https://www.nngroup.com/reports/teenagers-on-the-web/)
- [Tested] "Teens are overconfident in their web abilities, but they perform worse than adults, with lower reading levels, impatience, and undeveloped research skills reducing teens' task success and requiring simpler sites." — [NN/g, Teenager's UX](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-of-websites-for-teenagers/)
- [Tested] Teens "have a strong appreciation for aesthetics but detest sites that appear cluttered and contain pointless multimedia". NN/g "cautions against overusing interactive features just because you design for younger audiences". "Multimedia can engage or enrage teens, depending on its usefulness", and the best experiences "teach them something new or keep them focused on a goal". — [NN/g, Teenager's UX](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-of-websites-for-teenagers/)
- [Tested] "Teenagers dislike tiny font sizes as much as adults do. Even though this audience has sharp eyesight, most teens move too quickly and are too easily distracted to attend to small text." — [NN/g, Teenager's UX](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-of-websites-for-teenagers/)
- [Tested] Teens "often work on touch-enabled devices, making interactions that require precision such as dropdown menus, drag-and-drop, and small buttons difficult". "Rollover effects and small click zones are also problematic." "Display content in small, meaningful chunks with plenty of white space." Teens are "goal-oriented and expect websites and apps to be easy to use." — [NN/g, Teenager's UX](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-of-websites-for-teenagers/); [NN/g free report PDF](https://media.nngroup.com/media/reports/free/UX_Design_for_Teenagers_3rd_Edition.pdf)
- [Survey] Teens' notification load (Common Sense 2023): a median of 237 a day, about a quarter during the school day. — [Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/press-releases/teens-are-bombarded-with-hundreds-of-notifications-a-day)
- [Standard] WCAG 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum), AA: pointer targets at least 24×24 CSS px unless spacing, an equivalent control, inline text, user-agent control or essentiality applies. — [WCAG 2.5.8 (source)](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/guidelines/sc/22/target-size-minimum.html)
- [Standard; wording read via a verbatim reproduction] ICO Age Appropriate Design Code (Children's Code). Std 1: "The best interests of the child should be a primary consideration when designing and developing online services likely to be accessed by a child." Std 4 Transparency (paraphrase; the reproduction's wording may not match the Code exactly): privacy information must be prominent and in clear language suited to the child's age. Std 5 Detrimental use of data: "Do not use children's personal data in ways that have been shown to be detrimental to their wellbeing, or that go against industry codes of practice, other regulatory provisions or Government advice." Std 7: settings "must be 'high privacy' by default". Std 13 Nudge techniques: "Do not use nudge techniques to lead or encourage children to provide unnecessary personal data or weaken or turn off their privacy protections." — [ICO Children's Code (canonical; not fetched this session)](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/); wording checked in [Privacy-Data-Protection-Skills reproduction](https://github.com/mukul975/Privacy-Data-Protection-Skills/blob/9b2ef9eae161c00a17241d42a388571321b33e9f/skills/privacy/uk-aadc-implementation/SKILL.md)
- [Secondary] Summaries of the Code say it "challenges engagement-extension techniques — auto-play, personalised recommendations, and reward loops — where they may harm children" and "encourages positive nudges for wellbeing — e.g., prompts to take breaks". — [Ondato](https://ondato.com/blog/uk-age-appropriate-design-code/) and [SuperAwesome](https://www.superawesome.com/blog/the-uks-childrens-code-is-law-what-happens-next/), as quoted in [heylolo design-gallery research notes](https://github.com/Wladefant/heylolo-design-gallery) and [l3arn research notes](https://github.com/loveshotsmedia/l3arn_updated)

### Inferences
- Teens want an interface that looks good and is simple and fast. "Designing for young people" should not mean more colour, more animation or gamification. It should mean chunked content, readable text and forgiving touch targets.
- SubOne's users span 11–19, so some are adults (18–19). The safest approach is to apply Children's Code defaults to all student accounts. Teachers and mentors can opt into more detail.
- Implication for SubOne: Chunk every screen (cards, short sections, bullets) with generous whitespace. Do not add animation or "fun" effects for teen appeal (NN/g).
- Implication for SubOne: Keep body text at 16 px or more and secondary labels at about 12–13 px or more (inference). Avoid long uppercase labels (see the dyslexia guidance in Q7).
- Implication for SubOne: On phones, make touch targets at least 44 px where possible (24 px is the WCAG AA floor). Provide non-drag alternatives for board moves and timeline edits (a "Move to…" menu), and do not rely on hover to reveal actions.
- Implication for SubOne: No streaks, leaderboards of activity, "you haven't logged in" nudges, or reward loops for students. Positive nudges only, such as "You're done for today" or a gentle end-of-session summary (ICO Std 5 and Std 13 spirit).
- Implication for SubOne: Default student profiles and visibility to high privacy (ICO Std 7). Explain settings in short, age-appropriate words at the moment they matter, in the spirit of ICO Std 4.
- Implication for SubOne: Speed is part of calm for teens. Aim for instant-feeling navigation (optimistic updates, skeletons shaped like the content) rather than spinners (inference).

### Gaps
- The NN/g young adults (18–25) report and articles were not accessed. Whether young adults tolerate more density than teens is unverified.
- NN/g findings on teens' reactions to being "talked down to", and exact teen-versus-adult success rates, were not verified.
- The ICO Code's age bands and the exact detrimental-use examples (the full text of Standard 5 about "sticky" features, notifications and streaks) were not read from the primary text, because ico.org.uk was blocked. The engagement-feature bullet relies on secondary summaries.
- I found no teen-specific evidence on dark mode, motion sensitivity or badge anxiety.

## 7. Neurodiversity and cognitive accessibility (ADHD, autism, dyslexia, anxiety): W3C COGA and the UK Home Office posters; concrete rules

### Takeaway
The guidance is consistent and concrete. Keep layouts simple, consistent and predictable. Use simple colours and avoid bright, high-contrast palettes, while still meeting 4.5:1 for text. Write in plain language with short sentences and bullets, not walls of text. Use descriptive buttons. Do not make people remember things between pages. Limit interruptions and let users control them. Offer simplification, such as hiding non-essential features. For anxiety, explain what happens next, make consequences clear and let people check before submitting. ADHD-specific guidance is mostly the same attention-protecting rules (no blinking or auto-moving content, control over interruptions). I found no controlled studies of ADHD-specific UI in this session.

### Cited Findings
- [Guideline] COGA's eight objectives: (1) help users understand what things are and how to use them; (2) help users find what they need; (3) use clear and understandable content; (4) help users avoid mistakes and know how to correct them; (5) help users focus; (6) ensure processes do not rely on memory; (7) provide help and support; (8) support adaptation and personalisation. — [w3c/coga content-usable/index.html](https://github.com/w3c/coga/blob/main/content-usable/index.html); [COGA TR](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)
- [Guideline] COGA "Help users focus": "help users maintain focus by avoiding distracting the user from their task. If users do get distracted, headings and breadcrumbs can help orientate the user and help restore context when it is lost." User story: "As a user with an attention impairment and weak memory, I need to be able to avoid distraction and restore the context after I lose focus and come back to the task." — [W3C, Help Users Focus](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG2/supplemental/objectives/o5-user-focus/); [COGA user stories](https://www.w3.org/TR/2020/WD-coga-usable-20201211/user_stories.html)
- [Guideline] COGA patterns under "Help users focus": Limit Interruptions (o5p01), make short critical paths (o5p02), Avoid Too Much Content (o5p03; ≤5 main choices), and set task expectations (o5p04). Under "Use clear and understandable content" the patterns include clear words, literal language, succinct text and whitespace (o3p01, o3p04, o3p05, o3p10). The o5p02, o5p04 and o3 titles are inferred from file names only. — [w3c/coga content-usable/index.html](https://github.com/w3c/coga/blob/main/content-usable/index.html)
- [Guideline] COGA "Support Simplification": remove or hide non-essential features, less or simpler text, a "simplified 'reading' view that is available and easy to close". — [w3c/coga o8p03](https://github.com/w3c/coga/blob/main/design-guide/o8p03-complexity.html)
- [Guideline] UK Home Office poster, designing for users on the autistic spectrum (Do / Don't): "Use simple colours / Don't use bright contrasting colours" ("sensitivity to sensory information such as colours... can cause anxiety or pain. Use a colour contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for text and test it with your users to get the right balance"). "Write in plain English / Don't use figures of speech and idioms." "Use simple sentences and bullets / Don't create a wall of text." "Make buttons descriptive / Don't make buttons vague and unpredictable" ("Not knowing what will happen after clicking a button or link can cause users stress and anxiety"). "Build simple and consistent layouts / Don't build complex and cluttered layouts" ("Complex and cluttered layouts can be overwhelming... Make your layout predictable and consistent"). — [Home Office autism poster (source)](https://github.com/UKHomeOffice/accessibility-posters/blob/cce566a3dc15d0d359697132ae5764c1fc0b9066/autism.html)
- [Guideline] Home Office poster, designing for users with dyslexia: "Use images and diagrams to support text / Don't use large blocks of heavy text." "Align text to the left and keep a consistent layout / Don't underline words, use italics or write in capitals." "Consider producing materials in other formats / Don't force users to remember things from previous pages." "Keep content short and simple, make clear prompts / Don't rely on accurate spelling." "Let users change the contrast between background and text / Don't put too much information in one place" ("Many dyslexic users are sensitive to bright, high contrast colours. They can make words swirl and blur together."). — [Home Office dyslexia poster (source)](https://github.com/UKHomeOffice/accessibility-posters/blob/cce566a3dc15d0d359697132ae5764c1fc0b9066/dyslexia.html)
- [Guideline] Home Office poster, designing for users with anxiety: "Give users enough time to complete an action / Don't rush users or set impractical time limits." "Explain what will happen after completing a service / Don't leave users confused about next steps or timeframes." "Make important information clear / Don't leave users uncertain about the consequences of their actions." "Give users the support they need to complete a service / Don't make support or help hard to access." "Let users check their answers before they submit them / Don't leave users questioning what answers they gave." — [Home Office anxiety poster (source)](https://github.com/UKHomeOffice/accessibility-posters/blob/cce566a3dc15d0d359697132ae5764c1fc0b9066/anxiety.html)
- [Guideline] The Home Office set covers seven access needs (anxiety, autism, D/deaf and hard of hearing, dyslexia, motor disabilities, low vision, screen-reader users) and is licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 on condition that the Home Office name and logo are removed. — [UKHomeOffice/posters README](https://github.com/UKHomeOffice/posters)
- [Standard] ADHD and attention: WCAG 2.2.2's intent notes that people "with attention deficit disorders find blinking content distracting". MDN notes that blinking and flashing are "problematic for people with... ADHD". — [WCAG Understanding 2.2.2 (source)](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/understanding/20/pause-stop-hide.html); [MDN (source)](https://github.com/mdn/content/blob/main/files/en-us/web/api/web_animations_api/index.md)
- [Tested; title-level] Notifications increase inattention and hyperactivity symptoms, which are ADHD-like, even in the general population (Kushlev, Proulx & Dunn, CHI 2016). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858359)
- [Standard] Reading support. WCAG 1.4.8 (AAA): user-selectable colours, ≤80 characters, no justification, 1.5 line spacing. WCAG 1.4.12 (AA): layouts must survive user spacing overrides. — [WCAG 1.4.8](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/guidelines/sc/20/visual-presentation.html); [WCAG 1.4.12](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/guidelines/sc/21/text-spacing.html)
- [Practice] Linear's theme "contrast" variable produces high-contrast themes automatically, a precedent for contrast personalisation. — [Linear, part II](https://linear.app/now/how-we-redesigned-the-linear-ui)

### Inferences
- The rules that make SubOne calm for neurotypical users (consistency, fewer choices, controlled interruptions, restrained colour) are the same rules neurodivergent users need. Neurodivergent users also need control: to hide, pause, simplify and change contrast.
- The guidance on colour intensity has two sides. Autistic and dyslexic users may be distressed by bright, high-contrast palettes, but text contrast must still meet 4.5:1. The answer is restrained accent colour, no large saturated fills, and user-selectable contrast, not low-contrast text.
- Implication for SubOne: Add a "Focus mode" that hides the sidebar, badges, Chat previews and AI suggestions and pauses non-critical notifications for a chosen time (COGA Limit Interruptions and Support Simplification).
- Implication for SubOne: Keep the same layout skeleton in every section (header, then view controls, then content, with detail in the same drawer position) and the same component for the same job everywhere.
- Implication for SubOne: Write button labels as verb plus object ("Add part", "Log lap time", "Send to mentor"), never "Go", "Submit" or bare icons. Use plain English in UI copy, and provide a glossary for racing and engineering jargon.
- Implication for SubOne: Avoid walls of text in Playbook, rules check and AI answers. Lead with a one-line summary, then bullets, with details behind "Show more" (two levels at most).
- Implication for SubOne: Use sentence case for section and group labels (no ALL-CAPS eyebrow labels), no italics for emphasis, left-aligned text, and underlines only for links.
- Implication for SubOne: For consequential actions (spending money, submitting to judges, deleting a car version), show a check-your-answers or review step, state what happens next and when, and never use timers on students' input.
- Implication for SubOne: Show context so users don't have to remember it. Keep the item's name, section and parent visible in drawers, and restore the previous place and filters when users return.
- Implication for SubOne: In Settings, offer Theme (System/Light/Dark), Contrast (Standard/High), Motion (Full/Reduced), Density (Comfortable/Compact) and a "Reduce colour" option that turns status fills into outlines and icons.

### Gaps
- ADHD: I found no accessible controlled study of interface design for ADHD users. The guidance above is expert consensus (W3C COGA, WCAG intent text, MDN).
- The British Dyslexia Association Style Guide (often cited for 12–14 pt text, 1.5 line spacing, cream or off-white backgrounds and specific fonts) was not accessed, so its numbers are not included.
- Research on autistic users' colour preferences and sensory sensitivity in interfaces was not accessed beyond the Home Office poster.
- The full text of the COGA patterns for short critical paths (o5p02), task expectations (o5p04) and whitespace (o3p10) was not read.

## 8. Empty, loading and error states: how they can calm rather than alarm

### Takeaway
Calm states tell people what is happening, why, and what to do next, in plain and specific language without blame, and with one clear action. Empty states should communicate status, give in-context learning cues and offer a direct path to the key task. NN/g reports that in-context cues work better than forced tutorials. Error messages should be specific, positive and fix-oriented, and should avoid words like "invalid", "forbidden", "oops", "please" and "sorry" (GOV.UK). For anxious users, explain what happens next and let them review before committing. I could not verify loading-time thresholds in this session.

### Cited Findings
- [Guideline; Secondary] NN/g, "Designing Empty States in Complex Applications: 3 Guidelines". Empty states should communicate system status, provide learning cues, and offer direct pathways to key tasks. "In-context learning cues ... In most cases, this approach is generally more successful than forced tutorials shown to the user at initial use." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/empty-state-interface-design/), as summarised in [dork-labs/dorkos research](https://github.com/dork-labs/dorkos) and quoted in [link-sphere DECISIONS.md](https://github.com/BAECHAN/link-sphere_FE_NEW)
- [Guideline] GitHub Primer, empty states. Three types: feature not yet used, temporarily empty by design (for example no notifications), and error. "Use primary text to explain the purpose of the empty state, help users feel comfortable to engage with the content." Secondary text is optional and brief. Action copy is "brief and descriptive". Illustrations can "playfully engage" first-time users. For errors: "Vague messages like 'There was a problem' can be frustrating", and "If it's possible for the user to recover from the error, explain what went wrong and nudge them towards a path to recover." — [Primer empty states (source)](https://github.com/primer/design/blob/main/content/ui-patterns/empty-states.mdx)
- [Guideline] GOV.UK error messages: "Describe what has happened and tell them how to fix it. The message must be in plain English, use positive language and get to the point." Avoid technical jargon; avoid "'forbidden', 'illegal', 'you forgot' and 'prohibited'", "'please' because it implies a choice", "'sorry' because it does not help fix the problem", "'valid' and 'invalid'", and "humourous, informal language like 'oops'". "Use the same message next to the field and in the Error summary." Show the message in red with a red border that visually connects it to the question. — [GOV.UK Error message (source)](https://github.com/alphagov/govuk-design-system/blob/main/src/components/error-message/index.md)
- [Guideline] Home Office anxiety poster: explain next steps and timeframes, make consequences clear, let users check answers before submitting, keep time limits generous, and make support easy to reach. — [Home Office anxiety poster (source)](https://github.com/UKHomeOffice/accessibility-posters/blob/cce566a3dc15d0d359697132ae5764c1fc0b9066/anxiety.html)
- [Expert opinion] Calm Tech principle 6: "Technology should work even when it fails." — [Amber Case](https://caseorganic.com/post/principles-of-calm-technology/)
- [Guideline] Carbon: "Make sure there is always a way to communicate similar messages statically", so state changes should not depend on animation. — [Carbon motion (source)](https://github.com/carbon-design-system/carbon-website/blob/d8783ad2ae3b5e59c58f58311491f8a2c4e62631/src/pages/elements/motion/overview.mdx)

### Inferences
- Alarm comes from vagueness, blame, red everywhere and blocked progress. Calm comes from specificity, a single recovery action, colour confined to the problem, and work that is kept safe.
- Implication for SubOne: Every empty section should get one sentence on what will appear here, one primary action, and one learning cue. For example, Car/Build: "Parts you add show up here with their build status. [Add first part] · See how other teams track builds (Playbook)." No guilt copy such as "You haven't…".
- Implication for SubOne: Treat "temporarily empty" states as reassurance ("No risks flagged this week", "You're all caught up"), shown in neutral grey rather than celebratory colour.
- Implication for SubOne: Keep error copy specific and fixable ("Lap time must be in seconds, like 5.21"), with no "Oops", "invalid" or "please". Colour only the message and the field border red, never the whole card or page.
- Implication for SubOne: Make offline or failed sync non-alarming. Keep local edits, show a quiet "Saved on this device, will sync when online" status, and retry automatically (Calm Tech principle 6).
- Implication for SubOne: For loading, use skeletons shaped like the final layout and show nothing for very short waits. Say what is happening for long AI or 3D operations ("Checking your car against 2026 rules…"), with a cancel option (inference; see Gaps).
- Implication for SubOne: Rules-check failures in the Car section should read as a to-do list ("3 items to fix before scrutineering"), not as a red wall of failures, and each item should have its own fix link.

### Gaps
- Loading thresholds (NN/g's 0.1 s / 1 s / 10 s response-time limits, when to show spinners versus percent-done indicators, skeleton-screen research) could not be verified in this session. The loading implications above are inference.
- NN/g's error-message guidelines (for example "hostile patterns") were not accessed. GOV.UK guidance was used instead.

## 9. Light theme, dark theme, or user choice: which is better for comfort and readability?

### Takeaway
For people with normal vision, light mode (dark text on a light background) gives better reading and visual-acuity performance, especially at small text sizes, and dark mode shows no fatigue advantage. Dark mode helps some people with cloudy ocular media such as cataracts and is preferred by many. The evidence-based default is light, with a user choice of System, Light or Dark. Make dark themes dark grey rather than black, and let users adjust contrast (dyslexia and autism guidance).

### Cited Findings
- [Tested; Secondary] NN/g (Raluca Budiu, 2020), "Dark Mode vs. Light Mode: Which Is Better?", summarising Piepenbrock et al. (2013, *Ergonomics*): among younger (18–33) and older (60–85) adults, "light mode won across all dimensions" in visual-acuity and proofreading tasks. Older adults benefited less in acuity tasks. There was no significant difference in fatigue measures. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/), read from a [verbatim saved copy](https://github.com/digi5D-git/Pocket-git/blob/38b7c6d1b2ef2f78cfcdbf76835c3cc5941bdf62/Pocket/Slurped%20Pages/Dark%20Mode%20vs.%20Light%20Mode%20-%20Which%20Is%20Better.md)
- [Tested; Secondary] The same research group in *Human Factors*: "the positive-polarity advantage increased linearly as the font size was decreased", that is, "the smaller the font, the better it is for users to see the text in light mode". Participants did not perceive any readability difference. — [NN/g saved copy](https://github.com/digi5D-git/Pocket-git/blob/38b7c6d1b2ef2f78cfcdbf76835c3cc5941bdf62/Pocket/Slurped%20Pages/Dark%20Mode%20vs.%20Light%20Mode%20-%20Which%20Is%20Better.md); a second copy in [jgx0/docs](https://github.com/jgx0/docs)
- [Tested; Secondary] Dobres et al. (2017), glanceable reading (MIT AgeLab): "during daytime, there was no significant effect of contrast polarity, but during nighttime, light mode led to better performance". Small fonts were much harder to read in dark mode at night. — [NN/g saved copy](https://github.com/digi5D-git/Pocket-git/blob/38b7c6d1b2ef2f78cfcdbf76835c3cc5941bdf62/Pocket/Slurped%20Pages/Dark%20Mode%20vs.%20Light%20Mode%20-%20Which%20Is%20Better.md)
- [Tested; Secondary] Aleman et al. (2018, *Scientific Reports*): an hour of reading light-mode text produced "significant thinning" of the choroid, a marker associated with myopia development, more so in participants who were already myopic. This is a possible long-term argument for offering dark mode. — [NN/g saved copy](https://github.com/digi5D-git/Pocket-git/blob/38b7c6d1b2ef2f78cfcdbf76835c3cc5941bdf62/Pocket/Slurped%20Pages/Dark%20Mode%20vs.%20Light%20Mode%20-%20Which%20Is%20Better.md)
- [Tested; Secondary] Legge et al. (1985): "7 participants with cloudy ocular media had better reading rates with dark modes." — [NN/g saved copy](https://github.com/digi5D-git/Pocket-git/blob/38b7c6d1b2ef2f78cfcdbf76835c3cc5941bdf62/Pocket/Slurped%20Pages/Dark%20Mode%20vs.%20Light%20Mode%20-%20Which%20Is%20Better.md)
- [Guideline; Secondary] NN/g's recommendations: "in users with normal vision, light mode leads to better performance most of the time", but designers should "allow users to switch to dark mode if they want to", because of possible long-term effects of light mode, benefits for some visually impaired users, and personal preference. Long-form reading apps should offer dark mode, and apps should use the operating system's dark-mode setting. — [NN/g saved copy](https://github.com/digi5D-git/Pocket-git/blob/38b7c6d1b2ef2f78cfcdbf76835c3cc5941bdf62/Pocket/Slurped%20Pages/Dark%20Mode%20vs.%20Light%20Mode%20-%20Which%20Is%20Better.md)
- [Secondary; weak] A 2026 secondary write-up cites NN/g (2023, "Dark Mode: How Users Think About It and Issues to Avoid") for "the roughly even split of user preference and context-switching behaviour". It also claims dark mode can be worse for people with astigmatism because of halation, but I did not verify that. — [Ranzlappen blog post](https://github.com/Ranzlappen/website/blob/ff21508e066147f23573a382e4e694b6307161b5/_posts/2026-04-03-darkmode-vs-light-mode.md); [NN/g 2023 article](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode-users-issues/) (not accessed)
- [Guideline] Material: dark-theme surfaces should be "dark grey instead of black, which increases visibility for shadows and also reduces eye strain for light text"; higher surfaces get lighter in dark mode. — [Material Components Android Dark theme](https://github.com/material-components/material-components-android/blob/master/docs/theming/Dark.md)
- [Guideline] Home Office: autistic users may find bright contrasting colours distressing, so use at least 4.5:1 and test the balance. Dyslexic users should be able to change background and text contrast, and many are "sensitive to bright, high contrast colours". — [autism poster](https://github.com/UKHomeOffice/accessibility-posters/blob/cce566a3dc15d0d359697132ae5764c1fc0b9066/autism.html); [dyslexia poster](https://github.com/UKHomeOffice/accessibility-posters/blob/cce566a3dc15d0d359697132ae5764c1fc0b9066/dyslexia.html)
- [Standard] WCAG 1.4.8 (AAA): "Foreground and background colors can be selected by the user." — [WCAG 1.4.8 (source)](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/guidelines/sc/20/visual-presentation.html)
- [Practice] Linear derives light, dark and high-contrast themes from base, accent and contrast variables. — [Linear, part II](https://linear.app/now/how-we-redesigned-the-linear-ui)

### Inferences
- SubOne's current light, white canvas with near-black ink is the evidence-backed default, especially for dense UI with small numbers such as lap times, tables and chart labels, where the light-mode advantage is largest.
- Choice matters for comfort, preference, some low-vision users and evening use. Contrast control matters for dyslexic and autistic users.
- Implication for SubOne: Keep Light as the default for new accounts and add System, Light and Dark, following the operating system when the user picks System.
- Implication for SubOne: Build Dark from dark greys (not #000) with lighter raised surfaces, a desaturated team colour and desaturated status colours. Recheck 4.5:1 for text and 3:1 for status marks and chart lines in both themes.
- Implication for SubOne: In dark mode, avoid very small or thin text. Increase the weight or size of small numerals (lap times, axis labels) by one step, because the dark-mode penalty grows as fonts get smaller (inference from the *Human Factors* finding).
- Implication for SubOne: Near-black ink rather than pure black, and a very slightly warm off-white rather than stark blue-white, fit the autism and dyslexia guidance on avoiding harsh contrast while staying far above 4.5:1. Treat this as expert-opinion-level (inference).
- Implication for SubOne: Offer a "High contrast" theme generated from the same tokens (Linear's contrast variable) for low-vision users, rather than hand-maintaining a separate palette.

### Gaps
- No studies specific to teens on dark versus light mode were found.
- Readability in bright outdoor light (trackside on race day) was not covered by the studies accessed.
- The astigmatism and halation claim, and evidence for off-white or cream backgrounds (British Dyslexia Association guidance), were not verified in this session.
- The NN/g 2023 article on how users think about dark mode was only seen via a secondary citation.
